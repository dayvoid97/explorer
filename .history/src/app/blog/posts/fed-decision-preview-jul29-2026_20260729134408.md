In twenty-five minutes the Federal Reserve tells the world what it thinks about prices. We are publishing before it does, because a call made after the fact is not a call.

Here is ours: **Kevin Warsh does not cut today. He holds — and the tone points up, not down.**

## The setup

Futures markets are carrying roughly a **36% probability of a rate hike** into this meeting. That is an extraordinary number. Not a cut being priced out — a hike being priced *in*, at better than one-in-three. Positioning in the August fed funds futures contract has reached **967,136 contracts**, more than double where it sat in the second week of June, and above the previous record set in October 2024.

Translation: this is the largest bet the rates market has placed on a single Fed decision in years, and it is not a bet on easing.

The consensus headline will read "Fed holds." The consensus is probably right about the number and wrong about what it means.

## Why Warsh doesn't cut

Read what the man has actually said. At the ECB Forum on July 1, Warsh said plainly that **"prices are too high."** He allowed that inflation expectations were easing and then immediately said it was *still not good enough*. He dismissed any suggestion of tolerating a target above 2%.

Then read what he has refused to say. Warsh has stated repeatedly that he dislikes forward guidance and will not provide it. His first FOMC statement in June was conspicuously shorter than the Powell-era template. Asked directly at the ECB Forum whether markets could expect a July hike, he declined to answer.

A chair who wanted to cut has every incentive to prepare the ground. A chair who wants optionality to *tighten* says exactly what Warsh has been saying: nothing.

The Powell Fed sold predictability. Warsh is selling credibility, and he is charging the market a volatility premium for it. That premium is the whole trade.

## Oil is severely underpriced

Brent traded near **$87.53** on Tuesday, off about 0.9% on the day. WTI opened Monday near **$83.90**. Brent is up roughly **18.4% over the past month** and **22% year over year**, and July alone ran from **$72 on the 6th to $97 on the 24th** before backing off.

The reflexive read is that oil has already run. We think the opposite. That range — a 35% swing inside three weeks — is not a market that has priced geopolitical risk. It is a market that cannot price it, and is discounting hard because it does not know how.

Consider what crude is being asked to shrug off simultaneously: fragmented supply routes, a sanctions architecture that changes by the quarter, spare capacity concentrated in fewer hands than at any point in the shale era, and demand that has stubbornly refused to roll over on schedule. Every one of those is a fat right tail. None of them is in an $87 print.

Energy is the one asset class where the market is systematically paid to be short volatility and systematically wrong when it is. We think $87 Brent is a floor being mistaken for a ceiling.

## The dollar is too strong — and that is policy, not accident

The Dollar Index sits at **101.60**, its highest since June. Every strategist note will attribute this to hike expectations. That is the proximate cause, not the reason.

The United States is not passively receiving a strong dollar. It is defending one. Dollar primacy is the load-bearing wall of the post-1944 arrangement — the reserve status, the sanctions reach, the ability to run deficits that would break any other sovereign. A weaker dollar is cheaper for American exporters and catastrophic for American *leverage*. Washington has made its choice about which of those it values, and it made it a long time ago.

Warsh, whatever else he is, is a sound-money chair installed at a moment when the currency's global standing is contested. He will not be the one who lets it slip. Expect the dollar to stay stronger than fundamentals justify, for longer than models suggest, because the objective function is not the one the models are solving for.

That is a policy stance dressed as a market outcome. Trade it accordingly — but do not confuse it with equilibrium.

## Gold falls further

Gold is at roughly **$4,030–4,048** an ounce, down about 0.7%, sliding toward $4,000 as hike bets firm the dollar. Silver is off 1.5%.

Gold's entire bull case for two years has been the anticipation of easing, currency debasement, and a Fed that would blink. Warsh is the negation of all three. A hawkish chair who says prices are too high and refuses to guide is the single worst macro environment for a non-yielding asset priced in a strengthening dollar.

We think gold breaks $4,000 and keeps going. The positioning is crowded, the thesis is stale, and the marginal buyer was betting on a Fed that no longer exists.

## What to watch at 2:00

Forget the dot plot theater. Three things:

1. **Statement length.** June's was short by design. If July's is shorter still, Warsh is deliberately draining the guidance channel — bullish volatility, bullish dollar.
2. **Any language on the balance sheet.** Warsh's sound-money instincts run through the balance sheet, not just the funds rate. That is where the real tightening hides.
3. **The press conference on inflation.** If "prices are too high" reappears verbatim, the hike is not off the table for September. It is being loaded.
