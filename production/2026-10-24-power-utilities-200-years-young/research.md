# Research: "200 Years Young" — power and utility companies the data-center boom moved back up the life cycle

**Status:** preliminary research, for an article this week · **Prepared:** October 24, 2026 · **Data as of:** October 23, 2026 close

## 1. The premise and where it comes from

The framing paraphrases **Aswath Damodaran**, *"From Eyeballs to ARR: Buzzy Pricing Metrics and the AI Trade!"*, published **October 22, 2026** ([Substack](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/from-eyeballs-to-arr-buzzy-pricing)), in the "Industry grouping" part of his section on placing companies in the corporate life cycle. His point: investors use "technology = growth" and "utilities = mature or declining" as a shortcut, and the shortcut breaks. Some tech companies are mature or declining, and some power companies have used the data-center boom to become growth companies.

- **Attribution:** the article must credit Damodaran by name and link the post. Quote at most a short line; paraphrase the rest.
- **"200 years young":** this is **evian's 2026 campaign**, marking 200 years since its first bottling in 1826. Damodaran's post does not mention evian. The pairing is ours, and it works as a hook: something old being repositioned as young.

His life-cycle stages (for the article's framework): start-up, young growth, high growth, mature growth, mature stable, decline. A growth company reinvests more than it earns and needs outside capital; a mature company generates more cash than it can reinvest and returns it. That distinction is the test applied below.

## 5. What the data shows

1. **No company in the screen pays a dividend above the risk-free rate.** The best, Dominion at 4.10%, is below the 10-year (5.00%) and even the 3-month bill (4.22%), and Dominion's payout ratio is 93%. Utilities were long bought as "bond proxies" that yielded more than Treasuries. Today an investor buying any of them for income takes less than cash pays.
2. **The market prices these on growth, not income.** Forward earnings yields cluster at 4.4%–5.6%, roughly the 10-year yield. A stock with no earnings-yield premium over a riskless bond only makes sense if earnings grow. That's a growth valuation.
3. **The regulated utilities now consume capital, as growth companies do.** Every one has negative free cash flow. Capex runs at 36%–114% of revenue, and they fund the gap with debt (net debt at 5–8x EBITDA) and some new equity. In Damodaran's terms, reinvesting more than you earn is the signature of a growth company, not a mature one. The difference: regulated returns, not market returns, set the payoff.
4. **The independent power producers are a different kind of "young."** Constellation and Vistra carry low leverage (3.0x), positive free cash flow and token dividends (0.66%–0.67%). They sell existing, mostly nuclear, power to hyperscalers on 20-year contracts. Their growth comes from repricing old assets, not building new ones.
5. **Two archetypes emerge:**

| | **"Repriced" producers** (CEG, VST, NRG) | **"Rebuilding" utilities** (SO, DUK, AEP, D, ETR, XEL, NEE) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth engine | Selling existing generation at higher, contracted prices | Building rate base (grid + generation) for new load |
| Cash flow | Positive | Negative (capex > operating cash) |
| Dividend vs 10Y | 3–4.3 points below | 0.9–2.6 points below |
| Leverage | ~3x (CEG, VST) | 5–8x |
| Key risk | Power prices, contract renewals, behind-the-meter competition | Rate-case outcomes, credit ratings, equity dilution, data-center cancellations |

Exelon, a delivery-only utility with no generation, is the control: the lowest implied growth (+8%), the highest earnings yield, and the most "mature" profile.

## 7. Debt and credit

- **Leverage:** regulated utilities run 5.2–8.1x net debt to EBITDA. Constellation and Vistra run about 3.0x. NRG is 7.2x after the LS Power acquisition.
- **Duke:** S&P (July 2026) sees FFO-to-debt of about 14%–16%, with "ample cushion" against its downgrade trigger.
- **AEP:** one report says FFO-to-debt could fall below a 14% floor, with a first-half 2026 funding gap of about $3.3B. *Unverified; needs S&P or Moody's confirmation.*
- **Sector risk:** on **October 23, 2026**, S&P warned that data centers face downgrade risk from local opposition, moratoriums and cancellations (Bloomberg). That's the tail risk for the "rebuilding" utilities: debt-funded capacity built for load that may not arrive.

## 9. Working thesis

> **The data-center boom has split the "utility" label in two.** A handful of power producers, led by Constellation and Vistra, are being repriced as growth companies on the value of power they already generate, through 20-year contracts with hyperscalers. The large regulated utilities are behaving like growth companies in a different way: reinvesting more than they earn, funded by debt. Both are now valued on growth. Not one pays a dividend above a riskless Treasury bill. Investors who still buy utilities as bond substitutes are paying growth prices for what they think is safety. And the growth depends on data-center demand that local opposition and financing limits are already testing.

**Claims the article can make (with the numbers above):**
1. Every company in the screen yields less than the 3-month T-bill (4.22%) and the 10-year (5.00%).
2. The regulated utilities all have negative free cash flow, the reinvestment profile of a growth company.
3. Forward earnings yields about equal the 10-year, so investors are paying for growth, not income.
4. Constellation and Vistra are "young" through repricing; the utilities through building. Different risks, different winners.

**Counterpoints to address:**
- Regulated returns are lower-risk growth than tech growth. Is a ~5% earnings yield with 6–9% EPS growth a fair price for that?
- Dividend growth: utilities that grow dividends 5–7% a year can beat a fixed-coupon Treasury over time.
- Data-center cancellations, Texas interconnection pauses and behind-the-meter generation could strand capex.
