## What happens on Tuesday

The 2026 midterm elections fall on Tuesday, November 3, midway through President Donald Trump's second term. Midterms decide Congress, not the presidency — but they determine who writes the laws, approves the spending and confirms the appointments for the rest of the term. This year's vote will seat the 120th United States Congress, which governs alongside Trump through 2027 and 2028. Early voting is already well advanced: about 51 million ballots had been cast before Election Day, according to tracking compiled from 47 reporting states.

## The House: all 435 seats, a four-seat race

Every one of the 435 House seats is contested, because House members serve two-year terms. Republicans go in holding 218 seats to the Democrats' 214, with two seats vacant — a majority is 218, so Democrats need a net gain of just four seats to take the chamber and the Speaker's gavel. Unusually for a modern cycle, several states redrew their congressional maps mid-decade ahead of this election, a fight that began with Republican-led states and drew reciprocal Democratic redraws, reshaping a handful of districts before a single vote was cast. The Cook Political Report counts 17 House races as toss-ups.

## The Senate: 35 seats, and a tiebreak that matters

Thirty-five of the 100 Senate seats are on the ballot: the 33 regularly scheduled Class 2 seats, plus two special elections — in Florida, for the seat Marco Rubio vacated to become secretary of state, and in Ohio, where the seat JD Vance left for the vice presidency is contested by appointed senator Jon Husted and former senator Sherrod Brown. Republicans hold the chamber 53–47 (53 Republicans; 45 Democrats plus two independents who caucus with them) and are defending 22 of the 35 seats up, against 13 for Democrats. Democrats need a net gain of four seats to reach 51. A 50–50 split would not flip control: Vice President Vance breaks ties, so Republicans can lose a net three seats and still run the chamber. The most exposed incumbents sit in states that voted the other way in 2024 — Democratic-held Georgia and Michigan, both carried by Trump, and Republican-held Maine, the only Republican seat in a state Kamala Harris won. Cook rates seven Senate races as toss-ups.

## Governors: 36 states

Voters in 36 states also elect governors (39 contests including three territories). The two parties each currently hold 18 of the 36 state seats in play. Governors matter to the federal map as well as to state policy: they sign or veto the congressional maps drawn after the 2030 census and run the state machinery that administers federal elections.

## What is at stake

Control of Congress decides four practical things for 2027 and 2028. First, **spending and taxes**: the majority writes the appropriations bills and any extension or revision of tax policy, and a divided government raises the odds of shutdown standoffs and debt-limit brinkmanship. Second, **the presidential agenda**: a unified Republican Congress can move legislation on party-line votes; a Democratic House or Senate can block it outright. Third, **confirmations**: only the Senate confirms cabinet officials, judges and senior economic appointees, so the Senate result shapes the administration's personnel either way. Fourth, **investigations**: House and Senate committees controlled by the opposition gain subpoena power over the executive branch.

The campaign's closing weeks have been dominated by the cost of living — voters in October polling ranked affordability as the top issue — alongside the economic fallout of the war in Iran, immigration enforcement, and the parties' competing claims about corruption and democratic norms. Mid-October Reuters/Ipsos polling put President Trump's approval rating at 31% and Democrats ahead on the generic congressional ballot; both parties caution that midterm polling has missed before, and the president's party has historically lost House seats in midterm cycles — the pattern Republicans are trying to break and Democrats are counting on.

## Why markets are watching

For investors, Tuesday is a fiscal-policy event. A unified Congress points to faster movement on tax and spending legislation written by one party; divided government points to gridlock — historically a calmer backdrop for risk assets, but one punctuated by funding deadlines neither side can settle alone. The Senate result also decides who vets the next round of economic appointments. Results will arrive state by state after polls close Tuesday evening, with the House majority potentially clear by late night and close Senate races likely to run longer.
