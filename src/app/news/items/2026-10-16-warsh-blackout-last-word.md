## What happened

Fed Chair Warsh spoke overnight at the IMF gathering in Bangkok — his last scheduled appearance before the Fed's communications blackout begins tomorrow — and said nothing about rates. The paraphrased message, per the WSJ: he doesn't prejudge meetings and the Fed is no longer in the business of previewing decisions. Markets read the silence as dovish: October-hike odds were cut roughly in half, to about **13–15%** from ~26–30% on CME FedWatch.

He did offer one macro frame worth noting: the AI build-out is a "massive investment surge" — a future positive supply shock, though the path there will be "very bumpy."

## The data pushed back

This morning's prints told a hotter story:

| Print | Actual | Expected |
|---|---|---|
| Import prices (m/m) | **+1.1%** | +0.8% |
| Import prices (y/y) | **8.1%** — biggest since July 2022 | — |
| Export prices (m/m) | **+2.2%** | +1.0% |
| Industrial production (m/m) | **+0.7%** | +0.4% |
| Capacity utilization | **76.9%** | 76.5% |

Industrial production has now risen six straight months, up 2.2% year-on-year — the fastest pace in about four years. Manufacturing output rebounded 0.3%, led by semiconductors and related components.

## The tension

This is the whole October debate in one morning: a Chair who refuses to signal (dovish read) against trade prices running at multi-year highs and factories humming (hawkish read). The blackout starts Saturday, so there will be no more official guidance before the **October 27–28 FOMC meeting** — where policymakers will vote on September CPI (3.7%) and PPI (6.3% y/y) alone, since September PCE doesn't land until the 29th.

## Our take

The market is pricing the silence, not the data — which is exactly the kind of divergence that makes the next two weeks tradeable. If Warsh wanted to kill the hike chatter, he had every opportunity in Bangkok; instead he chose deliberate opacity. But import prices at 8.1% y/y are the kind of number that makes a data-dependent Fed uncomfortable sitting still. Watch the 10-year (now ~5.03%, lowest since late September): if yields keep drifting down while the data runs hot, something has to give — and it's usually the pricing, not the data.
