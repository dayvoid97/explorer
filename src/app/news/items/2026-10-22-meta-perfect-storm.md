## A seven percent day

Meta Platforms shares closed at $648.85 on Thursday, down 6.98 percent from Wednesday's $697.52 close — a fall that dragged on the wider market on a day when the S&P 500 was already down about 1.4 percent intraday. Three separate stories broke over the company inside a single news cycle: a $25 billion court penalty in New Mexico, shareholder approval of a new class of non-voting stock designed to fund the AI build-out without diluting control, and third-quarter numbers that showed capital spending outrunning operating cash flow. Each one was large enough to move the stock on its own. They arrived together, and that is what makes Thursday's tape so hard to read.

## Blow one: the $25 billion fine

On Wednesday, New Mexico judge Francis Mathew set a $25 billion civil penalty against Meta, following a jury verdict that found 26 of 29 statements by the company misleading and identified more than 43 million violations of the state's consumer protection law (Reuters). Judge Mathew found that a penalty of more than $219 billion was warranted under state law, before setting the figure at $25 billion. The state had asked for $35 to $40 billion; Meta had urged a cap of $3.45 billion.

The case is the Cambridge Analytica privacy litigation, filed in 2021 — not the separate child-safety proceedings against the company. Meta's chief legal officer, C.J. Mahoney, said the ruling "makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico," and the company plans to appeal (Reuters). An appeal means the $25 billion is a headline long before it is a cash outflow — but it is now the largest single number attached to Meta's legal exposure, and it landed the same evening as the earnings report.

## Blow two: Class C — growth without giving up control

Shareholders holding a majority of Meta's voting power approved, by written consent, charter amendments creating a new Class C common stock that carries no voting rights. The board has declared a one-for-one Class C stock dividend effective January 19, 2027 — economically a two-for-one split, delivered entirely in shares that cannot vote.

Meta's stated purpose is explicit: the Class C shares let the company finance AI growth, employee compensation and acquisitions without issuing voting stock. The structure leaves Mark Zuckerberg with roughly 60.6 percent of the voting power on about 13.4 percent of the economic interest. Zuckerberg may sell 20 percent of his Class C shares immediately — a provision framed as covering a potential tax liability — while the remaining 80 percent is locked to matching sales of voting shares, with restrictions lifting over four years. He has also committed to remain a California resident and the company's full-time leader for eight years.

Reuters framed the plan as renewing worries about the scale of Meta's spending commitments: the company is building a permanent, non-voting currency whose purpose is to keep the AI programme funded without ever asking public shareholders for control concessions again. Whether investors object to the governance or to the size of the chequebook it protects, the signal is the same — the spending is structural, not cyclical.

## Blow three: the capex machine eats the cash flow

The third-quarter cash flow statement is where the storm gathers its force (Meta's Q3 2026 release). Capital expenditure in the quarter was $34.91 billion — more than the $32.79 billion of operating cash flow the entire business generated. Free cash flow was negative $2.12 billion, against a positive $10.63 billion in the same quarter a year earlier.

Full-year 2026 capex guidance was narrowed to $135 to $145 billion — and note the direction of the edit: the floor was raised from $130 billion. Shareholder returns have been subordinated in plain sight: Meta bought back zero stock in the first nine months of 2026, after spending $3.33 billion on buybacks in the third quarter of 2025 alone. Headcount, meanwhile, fell 15 percent year over year to 66,775. The company is cutting almost everything except the build-out.

## The quarter underneath the storm

None of this is happening because the core business is failing. Third-quarter revenue was $64.60 billion, up 26 percent — a beat, and the company's seventeenth consecutive quarterly sales beat. Reported earnings per share of $3.83 include a $9.89 billion charge for the August multistate settlement of the teen mental health and social media litigation, an agreement that provides for total payments of up to roughly $18.59 billion; excluding the charge, earnings per share were about $7.40, and the reported operating margin of about 20 percent becomes roughly 35 percent. Fourth-quarter revenue guidance of $73 to $76 billion is not the guidance of a company in retreat.

Zuckerberg used the release to point at milestones toward "personal superintelligence," citing the launch of Muse and Muse Wonder, and noting that the Muse agent passed 10 million users in the US and Canada in about six weeks. The strategic story is coherent and, on the revenue line, it is working. The argument is entirely about what it costs.

## The attribution problem

So which blow knocked 6.98 percent off the stock? Anyone who gives you a single answer is guessing. The fine is appealable and may never be paid at $25 billion; the Class C plan is a governance story whose economic effect is a slow dilution of everyone except the founder; the capex figures were, in large part, telegraphed. The tape itself was falling — "Periodically, when they look at current earnings and the amount they're going to have to spend," investors grow uneasy that AI stocks are overpriced, as Rick Meckler of Cherry Lane Investments put it to Reuters. Meta did not fall on one fact on Thursday. It fell on the realisation that all of its facts now point the same way: the bills are getting bigger, the legal overhang is getting longer, and the structure is being rebuilt so that neither can be voted down.

## What happens next

Three dates and processes now carry the story. The New Mexico penalty goes to appeal, where Meta will argue the figure down from $25 billion — and where even partial success takes years. The Class C dividend becomes effective on January 19, 2027, converting the funding model from plan to plumbing. And the fourth-quarter print, guided at $73 to $76 billion of revenue, will be read less for the top line than for whether free cash flow turns back positive. Until one of those resolves, Meta's stock is a referendum on a single question its own shareholders can no longer vote on: how much superintelligence costs, and who pays.
