> **Key takeaways**
>
> **The Senate.** Democrats flipped **seven** Republican-held seats — Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina and Texas — and hold **at least 54 seats** with two races (Alaska, Mississippi) still uncalled. They needed four.
>
> **The House.** Democrats crossed the 218-seat majority line in the early hours of Wednesday. The AP tally this morning: **222 seats called for Democrats, with the party leading in 41 more**; final projections run from roughly 239 to 255 Democratic seats.
>
> **The turnout.** A record **57.5 million** early ballots (University of Florida Election Lab) and a preliminary turnout estimate near **50 percent of eligible voters** — presidential-cycle territory for a midterm.
>
> **The markets.** Nasdaq-100 futures fell as much as **2.4%** overnight, then recovered to trade higher by 3:13 a.m. ET. The 10-year Treasury yield fell about 5 basis points to **~4.92%** and the dollar index dropped **0.6% to 101.27**, a one-month low (Reuters).
>
> **The structure.** From January 3, 2027, Washington is divided government: a Democratic Congress, a Republican president, and a veto math — 290 House votes and 67 Senate votes to override — that neither side can reach. What changes is control of committees, confirmations, investigations and the funding calendar. What does not change is who signs the laws.

## The result, in numbers

Republicans entered Tuesday holding the Senate 53–47 and the House 218–214 with two vacancies. Democrats needed a net gain of four seats in each chamber. They cleared the Senate bar before midnight and the House bar shortly after.

**The Senate.** The AP called Senate control for the Democrats late Tuesday night, after Josh Turek's win in Iowa delivered the 51st seat. Seven Republican-held seats flipped in total:

- **North Carolina** — Roy Cooper defeated Michael Whatley for the open seat of retiring Sen. Thom Tillis. First Democratic Senate win in the state since 2008.
- **Texas** — James Talarico defeated Attorney General Ken Paxton in the most expensive Senate race in U.S. history. First Democratic Senate win in Texas since 1988.
- **Kansas** — Adam Hamilton defeated Sen. Roger Marshall. First Democratic Senate win in Kansas since 1932 — the longest drought in the country.
- **South Carolina** — Annie Andrews defeated the Republican incumbent, taking a seat Democrats had not held since Strom Thurmond switched parties in 1964.
- **Ohio** — Sherrod Brown completed a comeback, defeating Sen. Jon Husted in the special election for the unexpired term.
- **Iowa** — Josh Turek defeated Rep. Ashley Hinson for the seat of retiring Sen. Joni Ernst.
- **Maine** — Troy Jackson ousted five-term Sen. Susan Collins.

Democrats also held every exposed seat of their own that has been called: Jon Ossoff was re-elected in Georgia, Peggy Flanagan held Minnesota, Chris Pappas held New Hampshire, and Abdul El-Sayed won Michigan — becoming, respectively, the first openly gay senator (Pappas) and the first Muslim senator (El-Sayed) in U.S. history, per network reporting. Republicans held Florida's special election, West Virginia and Wyoming.

**Still counting.** Two Senate races remain uncalled. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom led Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by **43 votes** with 96 percent counted as of 3:48 a.m. (AP) — a margin inside recount territory in a state the president carried by 23 points in 2024. In Alaska, Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan leads, with the race too close to call. The final Senate composition is therefore 54–46 or 55–45, with 56 the outer Democratic bound if both remaining races break their way.

**The House.** Democrats reached 218 called seats in the early hours of Wednesday; the seat that put them over was Texas's 35th District, won by Johnny Garcia — a district redrawn in the 2025 mid-decade redistricting specifically to favor Republicans. In the AP's running tally this morning, Democrats have won 222 seats and lead in 41 of the uncalled races; Republicans have won 138 and lead in 34. Of the House votes counted so far, Democrats lead the national popular vote 59.1 million to 46.8 million, with roughly 88 percent of the expected vote in. Final-seat projections from the decision desks range from about 239 to about 255 Democratic seats — the high end would be the party's largest House majority since 2009. Hakeem Jeffries is in line to become Speaker when the 120th Congress convenes on January 3.

**The states.** The wave reached the governor's races. In Texas, Gina Hinojosa defeated three-term Gov. Greg Abbott — the first Democratic win in a Texas statewide race since 1994 (CNN). Democrats won both the governorship and the Senate race in each of Iowa, Minnesota and Ohio. Across 39 states, voters also decided 145 statewide ballot measures (Ballotpedia).

## What the voters were voting on

Financial Gurkha does not do political commentary; it does record what the data says moved the result, because that is what moves policy next. Three findings recur across the network exit polling and post-election reporting:

1. **Cost of living.** Pollsters found widespread voter anger over prices as the dominant issue of the cycle (USA Today). In the AP's House tally, the swing toward Democrats reached as high as 25 points in Texas districts and 23 points in Mississippi and Rhode Island (The Times).
2. **The Iran war and fuel prices.** The conflict ran through the campaign's final months. President Trump himself, in a Wall Street Journal interview, acknowledged that higher gas prices resulting from the U.S.–Iran conflict were in part responsible for the result. Brent crude settled around $104 a barrel on election night.
3. **Turnout composition.** The 57.5 million early ballots — 31.9 million in person, 25.7 million by mail — beat the entire 2022 midterm early vote by about 11.6 million. Prediction markets took more than $1.48 billion in wagers on the midterms (Reuters).

## The market night: a 2.4 percent fall, recovered by dawn

The financial reaction came in two distinct acts, and the second matters more than the first.

**Act one — the selloff.** As Senate results landed, futures dropped sharply, led by technology. Nasdaq-100 futures fell as much as **2.4%**, S&P 500 futures **1.4%**, and Dow futures more than 400 points at the low (Reuters, Bloomberg via network reporting). The selling was specific, not general: the exposed trade was the AI build-out. JPMorgan analysts had flagged the risk of an "AI sell-off on assumed higher regulation" (via NBC News), and the results gave that thesis concrete form — Democratic candidates for governor in Georgia and Texas, two of the busiest data-center states in the country, campaigned on temporary bans on new data-center construction, and Texas elected its candidate. The Nasdaq-100 had entered a technical correction on Monday, before a single vote was counted; the AI trade was already the market's most crowded and most questioned position after Friday's chip rout.

**Act two — the reversal.** By 3:13 a.m. ET, Dow futures were **up 0.46%**, S&P 500 futures **up 0.15%** and Nasdaq-100 futures **up 0.26%** (Reuters). Strategists quoted overnight framed the recovery in structural terms: divided government as a check on both parties — Lombard Odier's head of investment strategy called the configuration "the best possible outcome for markets." Chip stocks rallied into the morning even as the regulation question stayed open (Panmure Liberum, via Dow Jones).

**Rates and the dollar** moved in one direction all night. The 10-year Treasury yield fell about 5 basis points to roughly **4.92%**, touching its lowest level in nearly two months, as investors priced slower fiscal expansion and reined in wagers on 2027 rate hikes (Reuters). The dollar index fell **0.6% to 101.27**, a one-month low; the euro rose to $1.1258. Emerging-market stocks, down 1.8% early, recovered to close the Asian session higher. Cryptocurrencies fell, on the read that a Democratic Congress changes the path of market-structure legislation and increases scrutiny of the industry (Cointelegraph, Reuters).

The honest summary of the tape: the first reaction priced a regulation shock; the second priced a governance structure. The cash session today will arbitrate between them.

## What a Democratic Congress can actually do

This is the section that matters for portfolios, so it is worth being mechanical about it. From January 3, 2027:

**Fully in Democratic control, no presidential signature required:**
- **Committee chairs, hearings and subpoenas** in both chambers — oversight of the administration, of agencies, and of industries, including AI and crypto.
- **Senate confirmations.** With 54-plus seats, the majority controls the confirmation calendar for judges, Cabinet officers and regulators outright. This is the single hardest power that changed hands on Tuesday.
- **The floor agenda** in both chambers — what gets voted on, and therefore what the public argument of the next two years is about.

**Shared, deadline-driven, and where the real leverage sits:**
- **Appropriations.** Every funding bill must pass both chambers and be signed. Shutdown deadlines become negotiations in which Congress now holds one pen and the president the other. Bond markets priced exactly this on Tuesday night: fiscal gridlock as a restraint on spending growth is the core of the lower-yields trade.
- **The debt limit.** The same structure applies — a must-pass vehicle, two veto points, and a hard deadline.

**Beyond reach:**
- **New statutes over a veto.** Overriding requires 290 House votes and 67 Senate votes. No plausible count gets either party there. Major Democratic legislation — tax, healthcare, AI rules written into law rather than oversight — is positioning for 2028 unless the president chooses to deal.
- **The Federal Reserve.** The Fed's structure does not change. What changes is the hearing room. The next hard market dates are unchanged and near: the October jobs report on Friday, November 6, and the FOMC meeting on December 8–9, with rates currently at 3.75–4.00%.

**Sector files that genuinely re-open:** AI and data centers (oversight hearings, plus state-level moratorium politics now backed by a Texas governor-elect); crypto market structure (the legislation's path and the industry's oversight exposure both change); healthcare pricing (a top voter issue entering a Congress that campaigned on it — expect hearings and drug-pricing bills that test the veto); defense and energy appropriations (funding bills are where a Congress's priorities become numbers).

## What to watch from here

1. **Mississippi and Alaska.** A 43-vote margin in Mississippi means a recount timeline measured in weeks; the Senate's final number (54, 55 or 56 Democratic seats) sets the confirmation margin.
2. **The remaining House count.** Dozens of races, concentrated in California and New York, are still being counted. The difference between 239 and 255 is the difference between a working majority and a governing one.
3. **Today's cash session.** Whether the futures recovery holds when real volume arrives — and whether AI infrastructure names lead or lag it.
4. **Friday's jobs report** (November 6) and the **December 8–9 FOMC** — the data calendar reasserts itself immediately.
5. **January 3, 2027.** The 120th Congress is sworn in; the first funding deadline of the new configuration follows within weeks.
