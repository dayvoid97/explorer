> **Key takeaways**
>
> **Seven flips.** Democrats flipped seven Republican-held Senate seats — North Carolina, South Carolina, Texas, Ohio, Kansas, Iowa and Maine — after Republicans entered election night holding the Senate 53–47. Democrats needed a net gain of four (Reuters; AP).
>
> **54 seats, provisional.** AP tracker summaries on November 4 put Democrats at 54 seats to Republicans' 44, with Alaska and Mississippi still uncalled. Treat 54 as provisional, not certified: other trackers on the same morning ranged from 53 to 55 depending on the uncalled races.
>
> **One engine, seven transmissions.** In the AP Voter Poll of more than 100,000 voters, about 6 in 10 said higher-than-usual prices were more because of the president's policies, about 4 in 10 named the economy the most important issue, and about two-thirds said the president's policies had made the economy worse.
>
> **The farm ledger is in USDA's own forecast.** Net farm income is forecast at $158.4 billion in 2026, down 2.6 percent in nominal terms and down 5.5 percent after inflation. Fertilizer costs are forecast up 15.3 percent and fuel and oil up 28.8 percent (USDA Economic Research Service, September 2026 forecast).
>
> **Kentucky did not flip in the Senate.** Republican Andy Barr held the Kentucky Senate seat, about 55 percent to 43 percent. Kentucky's 6th House District did flip — Democrat Zach Dembo won a central-Kentucky district the president carried by 15 points in 2024. See the fact box below.

## The map, seat by seat

All results below are AP calls and unofficial returns as of November 4, 2026. No result is certified. State canvasses come later.

- **North Carolina — Roy Cooper (D) defeated Michael Whatley (R)** for the open seat of retiring Sen. Thom Tillis. AP called the race at 8:23 p.m. ET, the first flip of the night. It is the first Democratic Senate win in North Carolina since 2008 (Reuters).
- **Texas — James Talarico (D) defeated Ken Paxton (R).** AP called the race at 11:03 p.m. ET. Talarico led 5,445,121 votes to 4,249,752 in AP results data — a margin of 1,195,369 votes. It is the first Democratic Senate win in Texas since 1988, and the first Democratic win in a Texas statewide race since 1994 (AP).
- **Kansas — Adam Hamilton (D) defeated Sen. Roger Marshall (R).** AP results data showed Hamilton with 556,415 votes to Marshall's 503,240. It is the first Democratic Senate win in Kansas since George McGill in 1932 — a 94-year gap (AP).
- **South Carolina — Annie Andrews (D) defeated Sen. Darline Graham (R).** AP results data showed Andrews with 1,042,362 votes to Graham's 951,165. It is the first Democratic Senate win in South Carolina since 1998, in a state the president carried by 18 points in 2024 (Reuters).
- **Ohio — Sherrod Brown (D) defeated Sen. Jon Husted (R)** in the special election for the remainder of the term first won by JD Vance in 2022. AP results data showed Brown with 2,301,042 votes to Husted's 2,077,748. Brown, who served in the Senate from 2007 to 2025, serves through January 3, 2029 (Reuters).
- **Iowa — Josh Turek (D) defeated Ashley Hinson (R)** for the open seat of retiring Sen. Joni Ernst. AP called the race at 11:35 p.m. ET, the call that clinched the Senate majority. AP results data showed Turek with 686,487 votes to Hinson's 630,615. It is the first Democratic Senate win in Iowa since 2008 (AP; Reuters).
- **Maine — Troy Jackson (D) defeated Sen. Susan Collins (R).** In an AP board snapshot with the count incomplete, Jackson had 364,841 votes to Collins's 314,487. Collins, first elected in 1996, was seeking a sixth term (Reuters; AP).

The House was also called for Democrats by AP at about 1:30 a.m. ET on November 4, passing the 218-seat threshold. The exact size of the House majority was not yet final.

## The common drivers

Financial Gurkha does not do political commentary. It records what the data says moved the result, because that is what sets the policy agenda next.

**1. Prices, first.** The AP Voter Poll found about three-quarters of voters angry or dissatisfied with the direction of the country, about 6 in 10 disapproving of the president, and about 6 in 10 saying higher prices were more because of the president's policies. A second AP analysis found about two-thirds of voters nationwide said the president's policies had made the economy worse, and about 8 in 10 of that group backed the Democrat in their House race.

Those are national findings. Each flipped state expressed them through a local price:

- In **Maine**, it was heating oil. Reuters, citing the Energy Information Administration, reported heating oil above $6 a gallon nationally, up from about $3.50 a year ago, and diesel at a record above $6.50 a gallon. Reuters reported 54 percent of Maine homes use heating oil.
- In **North Carolina**, it was food, energy and health insurance. Cooper campaigned on the high cost of food and energy, and on healthcare: as governor he had completed a 2023 Medicaid expansion covering more than 700,000 North Carolinians, and he ran against Medicaid cuts in the 2025 tax and spending law that the Congressional Budget Office, via Reuters, put at about $1 trillion — described by Reuters as the largest cut in the programme's history — and against the curtailment of Affordable Care Act subsidies after which, KFF via Reuters reported, more than 200,000 North Carolina residents dropped coverage.
- In **Ohio**, it was the electricity bill. Brown pledged to crack down on data centre development and to require data centres to pay the full cost of their electricity, and his campaign called Husted, a former lieutenant governor who had recruited data centres to Ohio, "the face of data centers" (Wall Street Journal; AP). The causation question is more measured than the campaign: FactCheck.org, reviewing the underlying research, found data centre load growth contributed to significant price increases in the PJM grid in 2025, but that most of the observed price increases of the past few years are not due to data centres.
- In **South Carolina**, it was gas and groceries, plus a candidate question. About 6 in 10 South Carolina Senate voters in the AP Voter Poll said Graham — appointed in July after the death of her brother, Sen. Lindsey Graham — did not have the right experience to serve, and about 7 in 10 voters who doubted her experience voted for Andrews (AP).

**2. Farm math.** The United States Department of Agriculture's September 2026 forecast is the clearest single document in this election:

- Net farm income: $158.4 billion in 2026, down $4.3 billion, or 2.6 percent, from a revised 2025 estimate of $162.7 billion — and down 5.5 percent after inflation.
- Total production expenses: $492.8 billion, up $21.2 billion, or 4.5 percent.
- Fertilizer, lime and soil conditioners: $39.6 billion, up $5.3 billion, or 15.3 percent.
- Fuel and oil: about $21.6 billion, up $4.8 billion, or 28.8 percent — the largest percentage increase in the forecast.
- Direct government farm payments: forecast at $47.4 billion in 2026, up 69.8 percent from 2025. In other words, the headline income figure is being supported by federal payments even as market income falls.

Two qualifications belong in the same paragraph. Even after the decline, forecast net farm income remains above its inflation-adjusted 2006–2025 average if realised. And the distress is concentrated — by commodity, by region, and by balance sheet. Chapter 12 farm bankruptcy filings were 315 in calendar 2025, up 46 percent from 216 in 2024, the third consecutive annual increase (American Farm Bureau Federation analysis of U.S. Courts data).

**3. Fuel and the Iran war.** On November 3 and 4, the national average price of gasoline was $4.12 to $4.14 a gallon, against $3.05 on November 4, 2025, and the Energy Information Administration put on-highway diesel at $6.292 a gallon on November 3 (AAA; EIA). Reuters led its Maine report with the same driver: "The Iran war pushed up fuel and home heating oil prices — an especially salient issue in a largely rural state known for harsh winters." In Kansas and Iowa, the same fuel price arrived at harvest, in a combine and a grain truck, rather than in a furnace. It was the same line item in a different household budget.

**4. Tariffs.** Reuters reported that the president's trade war with Canada weighed on tourism, lobstering and logging in Maine; that tariff costs were a central campaign argument in Kansas; and that in Iowa, Turek argued tariffs had hit the soybean market directly. The export figure that frames the farm side nationally comes from North Dakota State University's Trade Monitor: $14.9 billion in U.S. agricultural export sales to China were lost from March 2025 to February 2026, attributed to China's retaliatory tariffs — including $6.8 billion in soybeans. That is an academic study, labelled here as such, not a government total.

Maine also provides the clearest test of a late tariff adjustment. On October 23, a presidential proclamation removed some Canadian products — including plywood sheets, some paper products, certain machinery and refrigeration equipment, and certain packing crates and boxes — from additional 50 percent duties, while replacing them in scope with other products including certain valves and carpets (White House fact sheet). The changes took effect October 30, four days before the election. The result in Maine was a Democratic win. What the sourced record does not contain is a Maine exit-poll question on the waiver itself, so this piece does not claim voters were polled on it. It records the sequence: relief on farm and business inputs arrived in the final week, while the price voters were paying that week — heating oil above $6 a gallon — was set by fuel, not by packaging.

## Fact box: Kentucky

> **Senate — held Republican.** Andy Barr (R) defeated Charles Booker (D) to succeed retiring Sen. Mitch McConnell. AP results data, with about 99 percent of precincts reporting, showed Barr with 784,867 votes to Booker's 612,349 — reported as about 55 percent to 43 percent (AP; Kentucky Lantern).
>
> **House KY-06 — flipped Democratic.** Zach Dembo (D) defeated Ralph Alvarado (R) in Kentucky's 6th District, central Kentucky including Lexington, the seat Barr vacated to run for the Senate. With 99 percent of votes counted, Dembo had 52.7 percent to Alvarado's 43.3 percent, according to AP reporting via WKYU. The district voted for the president by 15 points in 2024, and Dembo is the first Democrat to represent central Kentucky in the U.S. House since 2012 (Courier-Journal; WKYU).
>
> Any line that says simply "Kentucky flipped" is inaccurate. One House district flipped. The statewide Senate seat did not.

## What the new majority says it will do

House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries said House Democrats would focus on "driving down the high cost of living, lowering the price of groceries, gas and housing, and fighting to make healthcare affordable for every American." Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer said Democrats would focus on affordability, lowering costs, and oversight.

The specific first agenda reported by AP and Reuters is:

1. **Reinstate the Affordable Care Act premium tax credits** that expired at the end of 2025 — a stated first-100-hours vote in the House.
2. **Reverse the Medicaid cuts** in the 2025 law, including new work requirements. Reuters quantified the target in its reporting as undoing $900 billion in Medicaid cuts.
3. **Hold votes on tariffs and on the Iran war,** and, in incoming Senate Finance Chair Ron Wyden's words, "reassert congressional authority over our country's trade policy."
4. **Open oversight investigations,** using committee subpoena power that arrives with the gavels on January 3.
5. **Work through five affordability working groups** Jeffries set up before the election: healthcare, housing, groceries and goods, gas and utilities, and caregiving.

Measured against the flipped-state farm vote, the overlap is real in two places and indirect in a third:

- **Healthcare is the direct overlap.** Family farmers are very often self-employed and have had high rates of enrolment in the Affordable Care Act marketplace; the new majority's first two items are the marketplace credits and Medicaid, which also funds the rural hospitals those same communities use.
- **Tariffs are the direct overlap in intent.** The farmers who flipped described tariff costs on inputs and lost export markets. The new majority campaigned on undoing the tariff regime.
- **Fertilizer, diesel and farm credit are the gap.** On the public record searched for this packet, the stated Democratic agenda contains no farmer-specific deliverable on fertilizer supply, diesel cost, farm credit, or crop insurance. Those needs are quantified in USDA's forecast — fertilizer up 15.3 percent, fuel and oil up 28.8 percent — and, so far, they are answered in the agenda only indirectly, through the gas-and-utilities working group and the anti-tariff votes.

## What the majority can — and cannot — deliver

This is mechanics, not commentary.

**With its own votes alone, from January 3, 2027, the new majority can:** set both floors' schedules, chair every committee, hold hearings, issue subpoenas, block legislation by not passing it, and, in the Senate, block or slow confirmations — including judges, agency heads, and Federal Reserve governors (Reuters).

**Everything a farmer would feel directly needs more than that.** A new Farm Bill, a tariff-authority statute, restoration of the ACA credits as a standalone law, and reversal of the Medicaid cuts each need 60 Senate votes to break a filibuster, a House majority, and the president's signature.

**A veto override is not within reach.** Overriding requires two-thirds of both chambers — 67 Senate votes and 290 House votes (U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7). At 54 Senate seats, Democrats are 13 votes short in the Senate alone. Reuters stated the conclusion directly: Democratic majorities will not be large enough to override the president's decisions.

**The Farm Bill will be decided before the new majority sits.** The 2018 Farm Bill's third one-year extension expired on September 30, 2026 (Congressional Research Service). Federal funding runs to a December 11 deadline, and commodity and dairy support authority runs to December 31, after which permanent law from the 1930s and 1940s is the backstop. That window — November 9 to December 31 — belongs to the outgoing Republican Congress and the president. The new Democratic agriculture committees, whose chairs had not been announced in the reporting searched for this packet, inherit whatever the lame duck passes, or fails to pass.

The realistic route for the new majority's farm-relevant items is therefore the must-pass calendar: appropriations, the debt limit expected to need raising in 2027, and any Farm Bill extension — bills the president also needs, where terms can be traded. That is leverage. It is not enactment by majority vote.

## The responsibility test

The voters in the seven flipped states did not send a single, uniform instruction. A Kansas farm family itemised its costs on paper for CBS News. A Maine logger ran on heating oil and healthcare. A North Carolina governor ran on making things cost less. An Ohio senator who had just lost ran again on the electricity bill.

The common test underneath those campaigns is measurable: a Farm Bill passed, input bills lower than the 2026 ledger, health insurance a self-employed family can keep, and export markets reopened. From January 3, gridlock has two owners. This packet's state pieces measure the new majority against that ledger, state by state — starting with Texas and Kansas.
