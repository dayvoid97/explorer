No company in history had ever sold $100 billion of product in a single quarter. NVIDIA just did it twice in one report: total revenue of **$115.8 billion** for the third quarter of fiscal 2027, and inside it, a Data Center segment of **$107.6 billion** on its own. It did this in the same week its founder resigned, its board told the world he will not be reinstated, and a new interim chief executive ran her first earnings call. The leadership story is chaos. The operating story is the strongest set of numbers any company has ever published.

This piece separates the two. It walks the earnings, the cash flows and the balance sheet exactly as filed. Then it does the valuation work properly: the P/E against the current 10-year Treasury yield, and a discounted cash flow in the Damodaran style, using the assumption of 10 percent growth for a decade. The numbers first. Our view, clearly labelled, at the end.

## The quarter: $115.8 billion, and not close

Revenue of $115,844 million rose 103 percent from a year ago and 20 percent from the prior quarter, beating the company's own $108.0 billion guidance midpoint by $7.8 billion. Gross margin was 74.8 percent on a GAAP basis. Operating expenses were $9.2 billion. Operating income was $77.5 billion, a 66.9 percent operating margin. Net income was **$65.3 billion**, up 105 percent year over year, and diluted earnings per share were **$2.70** GAAP and $2.68 non-GAAP.

The mix matters more than the total. Hyperscale, the giant cloud builders, produced $60.1 billion. The second platform, AI Clouds, Industrial and Enterprise, produced $47.5 billion, up 195 percent from a year ago — the demand is broadening beyond the four or five buyers who carried the first wave. Edge Computing added $8.2 billion. Vera Rubin, the new platform, contributed about $20 billion in its first production quarter, which management called the fastest product ramp in the company's history.

For the nine months, revenue is $293.7 billion and net income is $183.3 billion. One honest adjustment belongs here: nine-month GAAP results include $25.5 billion of gains on equity securities inside other income. Strip those out, as the non-GAAP figures do, and nine-month net income is $164.4 billion. Still a record by any measure, but the clean operating number is the non-GAAP one, and we use both below.

Guidance for the fourth quarter is revenue of $130.0 billion, plus or minus 3 percent, in a 14-week quarter — with zero Data Center compute revenue from China assumed. On the call, interim CEO Colette Kress raised the fiscal 2028 revenue outlook to growth of about 75 percent, from 70 percent before. The one soft line in the guide is gross margin: 71.0 to 72.0 percent for the fourth quarter, down from 74.8 percent, as the Rubin ramp and memory costs bite.

## The cash flow statement: the part that cannot be staged

Earnings can be argued with. Cash flow is harder to fake, and this is where the quarter is strongest.

Operating cash flow in the third quarter was **$48.3 billion**, double the prior quarter's $24.1 billion. That is 74 percent of net income — the conversion is held down by a deliberate working-capital build: receivables absorbed $18.1 billion in the quarter and inventories $8.1 billion, as the company stocks up for Rubin. Capital expenditure was light, as it always is for NVIDIA: $4.4 billion. **Free cash flow was $43.8 billion in the quarter and $113.7 billion for the nine months.**

Where did the cash go? Three places, all visible in the statement:

- **Shareholders.** $28.1 billion returned in the quarter alone, a record: $22.1 billion of buybacks and $6.0 billion of dividends. The board added $150 billion to the repurchase authorization in September, leaving $227.3 billion. The dividend is now $0.25 a share quarterly, payable December 23.
- **The ecosystem.** NVIDIA bought $58.1 billion of equity securities in nine months, $15.7 billion of it in the third quarter — stakes in the very companies that buy its chips, including its $10 billion participation in Anthropic's IPO private placement. This is the circular-financing question critics raise, and it is real; it is also disclosed, in the open, in the cash flow statement.
- **The balance sheet.** Cash rose to $29.1 billion even after all of the above.

## The balance sheet: a fortress with footnotes

At October 25, 2026, total assets were **$372.2 billion**, up from $206.8 billion in January. Liquid assets — cash of $29.1 billion, marketable debt securities of $24.4 billion and marketable equity securities of $44.2 billion — total **$97.7 billion**, against total debt of just $32.4 billion, all of it long-term. Shareholders' equity is $266.7 billion. On any conventional measure this is one of the strongest balance sheets in the world.

The footnotes are where the risk has moved, and objectivity requires naming them:

- **Receivables are $81.0 billion**, or 64 days of sales, up from 60 days. Two customers account for 18 percent and 17 percent of that balance. Four direct customers produced 48 percent of nine-month revenue.
- **Inventories are $39.7 billion**, up from $31.6 billion in one quarter, built ahead of the Rubin ramp.
- **Supply commitments are $390 billion**, and total future commitments reach $518 billion. Maximum guarantee exposure is $118 billion, dominated by about $105 billion of credit support for the PORTS-Pike campus in Ohio, where NVIDIA infrastructure will sit under 20-year leases to OpenAI beginning in fiscal 2029. No payments have been made under any guarantee to date.

None of this is hidden. All of it is the price of being the arms dealer and the bank for the same war.

## The P/E story: the earnings yield now beats the 10-year

Now the valuation. NVIDIA closed Tuesday, November 17 at **$155.46**, before the report. Trailing twelve-month diluted earnings per share, built from the four reported quarters, are **$9.31 GAAP** and $8.49 non-GAAP. Trailing net income is $226.3 billion on a market value of about $3.75 trillion.

- **Trailing P/E: 16.7 times GAAP, 18.3 times non-GAAP.**
- **Earnings yield: 6.0 percent GAAP, 5.5 percent non-GAAP.**
- **The 10-year Treasury yield: 5.11 percent** at Tuesday's close.

Read that again, because it is the centre of the story. For most of the AI run, NVIDIA's earnings yield sat far below the risk-free rate — you were paying for growth and nothing else, and a year ago the multiple was above 40 times. After a year in which earnings more than doubled and the share price fell about 17 percent, the arithmetic has flipped: the trailing earnings yield on this stock is now **higher than the yield on a 10-year Treasury**, by about 0.9 of a percentage point on GAAP earnings and 0.35 of a point on the clean non-GAAP figure. The market is no longer asking you to accept a negative equity premium for owning the most profitable company on earth. On the fourth-quarter guide alone, annualised earnings put the forward multiple near 14 times.

A 16.7 P/E does not make a stock cheap. It makes the argument specific: you are not paying for perfection any more. You are paying for growth that has to be good, not miraculous. Which raises the only question that matters — how much growth is actually in the price? That is what a DCF is for.

## The Damodaran DCF: 10 percent for a decade

Aswath Damodaran's method is a discipline: turn the story into numbers, discount the cash, and state every assumption where readers can attack it. Here are ours, in the open:

- **Base free cash flow: $150 billion** for fiscal 2027 — the nine-month actual of $113.7 billion plus a deliberately conservative fourth quarter, below what the $130 billion revenue guide implies.
- **Growth: 10 percent a year for ten years** — the assumption we were asked to test. Note what it means: it assumes the current hyper-growth ends almost immediately and the company compounds at roughly nominal-GDP-plus for a decade, reaching about $389 billion of annual free cash flow in year ten.
- **Terminal growth: 3.5 percent** thereafter.
- **Cash and investments added back: $130.9 billion** — the $97.7 billion liquid pile plus $65.5 billion of non-marketable securities, less $32.4 billion of debt.
- **Shares: 24.19 billion** diluted.
- **Discount rate: shown four ways**, because this is where the fight is. A full cost-of-equity build — the 5.11 percent 10-year as the risk-free rate, a 4.5 percent equity risk premium, and NVIDIA's beta of about 1.7 — gives about 12.8 percent. We also show 10, 11 and 12 percent.

**The results, per share, at 10 percent growth for a decade:**

- Discounted at 10 percent: **$166** — about 7 percent above the $155.46 price
- Discounted at 11 percent: **$143** — about 8 percent below
- Discounted at 12 percent: **$125** — about 20 percent below
- Discounted at 12.8 percent (the full cost-of-equity build): **$113** — about 27 percent below

**Sensitivity at an 11 percent discount rate:** 6 percent growth gives $108; 8 percent gives $124; 10 percent gives $143; 12 percent gives $164; 15 percent gives $203.

**Reverse the question — what growth is the price asking for?** At an 11 percent discount rate, $155.46 implies free cash flow growth of about **11.2 percent a year for a decade**. At the strict 12.8 percent cost of equity, it implies about 14.7 percent. Set that against the record: revenue up 103 percent this quarter, a $130 billion guide for next quarter, and management guiding fiscal 2028 revenue up about 75 percent. The market price does not assume the boom continues. It assumes the boom ends and a strong, ordinary compounder remains. Investors are free to find even that too optimistic — but they should argue with 11 to 15 percent, not with the fantasy multiple of a year ago.

## Our view

We will say it plainly, because the numbers above earn us the right to an opinion: this is a solid company, with great earnings, selling the one input every serious institution on the planet currently needs to make progress. Intelligence is being built the way electricity was built, and NVIDIA is the turbine maker. The earnings are real, the cash conversion is real, the balance sheet is a fortress, and after the repricing of the last two months the stock's earnings yield stands above the 10-year Treasury for the first time in this cycle. On the evidence of this report, we think the case for owning NVIDIA makes sense — not at any price, and the DCF table above shows exactly where the price stops working, but at this price, with these numbers.

The risks are not imaginary, and the largest is the one management cannot control. Regulation is coming for the AI buildout — it has already started. Export controls have zeroed out China data-centre revenue in the guide; new Commerce Department controls landed this very week; the board has had to stand up a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee; Reuters has reported an SEC probe into the company's disclosures following the Huang resignation, which the company has not confirmed. As the buildout inevitably slows from its current frantic pace, the regulatory hurdles will multiply — on exports, on power, on competition, on the financing web described above.

Our judgement is that this is a reason for vigilance, not for exit. The companies that own the indispensable layer of a buildout do not go quietly when the rules tighten; they hire, they lobby, they redesign products around every rule, and they keep competing. NVIDIA has already done this through three rounds of export controls. The demand underneath — every lab, every cloud, every government trying not to be left behind — does not disappear because a form gets harder to file.

Earnings are just a report. But some reports describe a business so strong that the argument moves from whether the numbers are real to whether the world will let them continue. That is where NVIDIA stands tonight.

## What could go wrong

- **Margins.** The fourth-quarter gross margin guide of 71 to 72 percent is a real step down. If memory prices and ramp costs keep margins there rather than recovering, the earnings path flattens.
- **Concentration and the financing web.** Four customers at 48 percent of revenue, $81 billion of receivables, $518 billion of commitments and $118 billion of guarantees mean NVIDIA's fate is tied to its customers' ability to keep raising money.
- **Leadership.** The company is being run by an interim CEO while a search firm looks inside and outside; the board's investigation continues, and the founder will not be reinstated, the chairman said on the call. Execution through a platform ramp, under that cloud, is not guaranteed.
- **The discount rate itself.** At a 5.11 percent 10-year, the strict DCF says the price needs nearly 15 percent growth for a decade. If the long rate rises further, that bar rises with it.
