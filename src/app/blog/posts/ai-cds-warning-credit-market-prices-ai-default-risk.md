## 1. The tape: records everywhere you look

A credit default swap is insurance on a company's debt. The buyer pays a running spread — quoted in basis points, hundredths of a percent — and gets paid if the borrower defaults. When the spread widens, the market is charging more for the same risk.

This month, it widened everywhere at once:

| Name | 5-yr CDS (latest) | Earlier reference | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CoreWeave** | **~1,090bp** | ~390bp a year ago; intraday peak 1,194.5bp on Oct 15 | Distressed; ~60% implied 5-yr default probability |
| **SoftBank** | 424bp | +24bp in one day (Oct 15) | AI's most leveraged proxy |
| **Oracle** | **322bp** (Oct 19) | 144bp in January; 288bp on Oct 15 | Steepest big-name deterioration |
| **SpaceX** | 226bp | 110bp at CDS debut, late June | Doubled in under four months |
| **Broadcom** | 136bp | Record high | Custom-silicon debt load |
| **Meta** | 108bp | Record high | $108K/yr insures $10M |
| **Nvidia** | 101bp | ~90bp mid-October | Even the safest name, at a record |
| **Alphabet / Amazon / Microsoft** | All-time peaks | Basket avg ~100bp (Sept) | Up from under 40bp a year ago |
| *CDX IG index (benchmark)* | *~62bp* | — | The "normal" price of IG insurance |

Two features stand out. First, the **levels**: three of the most profitable companies on Earth (Meta, Nvidia, and the hyperscaler basket) are paying record prices for default insurance — not because anyone expects them to default tomorrow, but because the stock of their debt has exploded. Second, the **dispersion**: the gap between Nvidia at ~101bp and CoreWeave at ~1,090bp is the market sorting the AI complex into who owns the cash flows and who rents them.

## 2. The trend: debt first, questions second

CDS does not move in a vacuum. It follows issuance, and issuance has been historic:

| Period | Hyperscaler gross bond issuance |
|---|---|
| 2024 | ~$17 billion |
| 2025 | ~$109 billion |
| H1 2026 | ~$194 billion |

Across all AI-linked borrowers, Morgan Stanley estimates **$466 billion** of issuance so far in 2026, against $101 billion in the same stretch of 2025, with the year's AI debt total on track near $570 billion. Goldman Sachs sees the five hyperscalers alone issuing $250 billion this year and $400 billion next.

The market's digestion is visibly worsening. Order cover on hyperscaler deals fell from roughly **5x in February to under 2x by July** (Apollo). Even Alphabet pays more each trip: its 10-year new-issue spread went from **63bp in February to 85bp in August**. AI issuers as a group trade around **115bp** of spread versus ~78bp for the investment-grade index.

Oracle is the cleanest trend line in the complex: **144bp in January → ~210bp in September → 261bp in early October → 288bp (a record, +26bp overnight) on October 15 → 322bp on Monday.** S&P has cut Oracle to **BBB-**, the final rung of investment grade, and its stock is down roughly 35% this year. When the second-most-indebted name in the trade also becomes its fastest-deteriorating credit, the CDS market notices first.

Trading volume confirms the focus. Single-name CDS on the seven most-watched AI borrowers changed hands at **more than $9 billion a week** in September, up from under $2 billion a week in July. Total US corporate CDS index volume in 2026 has already exceeded all of 2025.

## 3. CoreWeave: anatomy of the distress end

CoreWeave is the purest expression of the debt-funded AI model: borrow against GPUs, rent the compute, refinance the difference. The credit market's verdict, as of this week:

- 5-year CDS **~1,090bp** (record), after an intraday print of **1,194.5bp** — up ~164% year over year
- 2031 bonds yielding **15–16%**, unsecured notes at **76.5–81 cents** on the dollar — five straight weekly declines
- **$35.6 billion** of debt principal, roughly **$50 billion** in total obligations; **$4.4 billion** matures by end-2026, **$6.2 billion** in 2027
- S&P rating: **B+**. Company guidance implies **negative free cash flow until 2030**

The defense is not nothing. The backlog is real — more than **$100 billion** of contracted, take-or-pay revenue. Nvidia backstops unsold capacity through 2032, and CEO Jensen Huang said publicly this week that CoreWeave's financing costs are falling and no backstop is needed. JPMorgan's read is that the debt sits largely in secured, project-level vehicles, so a default would "sting, not sink" the lending banks.

The tension is timing. Backlog revenue arrives over years; interest and maturities arrive every quarter. At a 16% refinancing cost, the model needs everything to go right — in the same week that, as we documented in [Record AI Profits, Worst Tech Week Since June](https://financialgurkha.com/blog/record-ai-profits-worst-tech-week-cost-of-capital), CoreWeave's stock fell more than 22% and its peers (Nebius −6.4%, IREN −5.5%, Applied Digital −4.2%) fell with it. In the language of that report: the sector episode is synchronized across AI equity *and* AI credit. That is new, and it is what the CDS tape is really saying.

Some of this is the Treasury floor — the 30-year yields 5.63% and the 10-year 5.27% — which lifts every corporate yield mechanically. But note the prices: Microsoft's 2060s at **49 cents** and Oracle's 2065s at **73.69** are not what fortress balance-sheet paper looked like two years ago. And the off-balance-sheet layer is repricing too: bonds funding Meta's Hyperion data center through a special-purpose vehicle traded near **91 cents**, yielding up to **7.55%**.

## 5. The honest nuance: hedging is not the same as fear

An intellectually honest reading has to include the mechanical bid. Equity and multi-asset managers, facing expensive stock puts on volatile AI names, have discovered that CDS is the **cheap hedge** — Morgan Stanley Investment Management's Jim Caron makes exactly this case. Every hedging program that buys protection pushes the spread wider whether or not the buyer's default view changed. Part of the record prints at Meta and Nvidia is this flow, not fresh information about Meta or Nvidia.

There is also a benign version of the whole story: the CDS market is doing its job early. Spreads at 100bp for cash-rich hyperscalers remain trivially cheap insurance in absolute terms; the IG index at ~62bp says the broader credit market is calm; and Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid notes this is "not the first time we've had a sizeable market wobble on the back of AI fears."

Where the nuance runs out is concentration. The widening is not random — it is worst precisely where leverage, negative free cash flow, and refinancing walls coincide (CoreWeave, Oracle), and it is arriving alongside the first synchronized equity-credit selloff in the complex. Hedging flow explains the level better than it explains the pattern.

## 6. The systemic question: weakest link, or sting?

The two live arguments:

**The weakest-link case** (Polus Capital's Robert Dafforn; S&P Global Market Intelligence's Gavan Nolan): the AI financing web is interlinked — hyperscalers guarantee neocloud revenue, neoclouds borrow against the guarantees, SPVs hold the assets, banks and private credit hold the SPV paper. That "complexity premium" is what CDS is now charging. In a web, the failure of one node reprices every node connected to it.

**The containment case** (JPMorgan; Apollo's Torsten Sløk, in a different register): the biggest debts sit on the strongest balance sheets in corporate history; the riskiest debt is secured against physical assets inside bankruptcy-remote vehicles; and the hyperscalers could stop issuing tomorrow and fund capex from cash flow at a slower pace. Sløk's warning is narrower — that credit metrics are flattered by the cycle — not that the structure breaks.

Our read, consistent with the tail-episode lens we applied to last week: this is still a **sector episode, not a systemic one**. Treasuries rallied during the AI selloff; the Dow rose; the IG index is calm. The flip condition is the same one we flagged then: AI credit stress migrating into broad high-yield and bank balance sheets while spreads widen everywhere at once. The CDS market — with volume at records and the weakest names already distressed — is now the fastest place that migration would show up.

## The bottom line

The equity market still prices the AI boom on demand: record profits, record orders, record capex. The credit market has started pricing it on **financing**: who borrowed, at what rate, against which cash flows, maturing when.

Both can be right for a while. TSMC can print a record quarter while CoreWeave's insurance costs 1,090bp. But the CDS tape is now the most honest real-time referendum on the buildout — liquid, rising in volume, and unsentimental. It says the boom's safest names are merely fine, its leveraged names are deteriorating fast, and its weakest name is priced like a coin-flip.

> **The question is no longer whether AI demand is real. It is whether the credit funding it stays patient. The CDS market just raised its hand.**
