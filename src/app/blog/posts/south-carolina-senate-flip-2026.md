## How this seat came open in July

This race was not on either party's competitive map at the start of the year. It was created by a death, an appointment, and a divisive nomination fight — in that order.

Sen. Lindsey Graham died suddenly on July 11 at age 71, weeks after winning the June 9 Republican primary for a new term. President Trump lobbied Governor Henry McMaster, who on July 13 appointed Graham's sister, Darline Graham, to serve the remainder of the term. Trump then backed her for the full term.

Darline Graham, 62, was a little-known state employee who had never held elected office (Reuters). She won the nomination only after beating Rep. Ralph Norman in an August 25 special runoff, and Reuters reports that divisions from that runoff lingered into the general election. During the primary she acknowledged she was "not that informed on national security" (Reuters).

Against her, Democrats nominated Annie Andrews, a pediatrician. Reuters frames her campaign in three parts: healthcare access and affordability, and fighting corruption. Andrews is also the first woman ever *elected* to the Senate from South Carolina — Graham was the first woman to represent the state in the Senate, by appointment (AP).

## The experience question, measured

Candidate-specific explanations are often asserted after an upset. In this race, the explanation was measured on election day itself.

AP Votecast — AP's survey of the electorate — found that about **6 in 10 South Carolina Senate voters said Darline Graham did not have the right experience to serve in the Senate**. Most Republican voters believed she did have the right experience. Among voters who doubted her experience, about **7 in 10 voted for Andrews**.

That split describes the structure of the result. Andrews did not need to convert the Republican base. She needed the voters — including voters otherwise voting Republican down the ballot — who separated this one appointed candidate from the rest of the ticket. The experience question gave those voters a candidate-specific reason, distinct from any view of the national parties.

Andrews also outraised Graham by $22 million (Reuters), an unusual position for a challenger in a state this Republican, and one that let her campaign define the appointment before the autumn rescue spending arrived. Republican outside groups ultimately poured $27 million into holding the seat, against $24 million from Democratic groups (Reuters).

## The national wave underneath it

The experience gap was the race-specific driver. It was not the only driver, and on its own it does not explain why a seat in an 18-point Trump state was within reach at all.

Reuters' account of the result places Andrews' win inside a broader national pattern: the president's approval rating hit a record low amid voter discontent with the war in Iran, high diesel prices, and the cost of living — conditions Reuters says "put once solidly Republican seats into play." Andrews' win, in Reuters' wording, "embodies the broader rejection of Trump and his handling of the economy."

The national AP Voter Poll, conducted across more than 100,000 voters, found the same backdrop in every flipped race: about three-quarters of voters said they were angry or dissatisfied with the direction of the country, about 6 in 10 disapproved of the president, and about 6 in 10 said higher-than-usual prices were more because of the president's policies. About 4 in 10 named the economy the most important issue.

The closing exchange of the South Carolina campaign captured both layers in one moment. At a late rally, Graham called Andrews "batshit crazy." Andrews replied by asking whether it is crazy "to want cheaper gas and groceries" (Reuters). One candidate was arguing about the other candidate. The other was arguing about prices.

## What the result does and does not show

Three limits belong on the record.

**First, the margin.** 4.6 points in the two-candidate count, in a state the president carried by 18 points two years earlier, is a swing of more than 20 points against the appointed incumbent — and it is still a single-digit win, not a realignment measurement. A third candidate took about 1% of the vote, so full-field shares are slightly lower than the two-candidate figures above.

**Second, the candidate was the variable.** South Carolina voters did not reject Republican candidates generally on Tuesday; the data point that separates this race is specific to this nominee — the 6-in-10 experience finding. A different Republican nominee, elected rather than appointed, is a different race. The data does not test that counterfactual; it only records what happened with this one.

**Third, nothing here is certified.** The figures above are AP calls and unofficial returns as of November 5. Certification follows the state canvass.

## Why it matters beyond South Carolina

South Carolina was the seventh Senate flip in a night that moved control of the chamber. Its mechanics are the packet's clearest case study in how an appointment converts a safe seat into a contested one: an unelected incumbent, a shortened and divided nomination, a challenger with a professional identity outside politics, and a national cost-of-living wave that made the state reachable in the first place.

Neither factor alone produces this result in the data. In a normal cost environment, an 18-point state absorbs a weak nominee. With a strong elected incumbent, a cost wave produces a close loss, not a flip. South Carolina on Tuesday had both — and the AP Votecast experience number, 6 in 10, is the measurement that connects them.
