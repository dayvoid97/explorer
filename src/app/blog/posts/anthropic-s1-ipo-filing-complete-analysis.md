**The seven things that matter:**

2. **Compute efficiency drove the turn to profit.** Anthropic spent $2.51 on compute for every dollar of revenue in Q1 2025. By Q2 2026 it spent $0.48.
3. **The giant net loss is mostly an accounting entry.** About $81 billion of the $80.7 billion first-half loss comes from marking up convertible notes and other instruments that rise in value as Anthropic becomes more valuable.

6. **Public shareholders buy economics, not control.** A purpose trust elects the board majority, and the seven co-founders hold 50.1% of shareholder votes through a single share.
7. **The bet rests on agents.** The filing’s growth case is the “Virtual Collaborator”: AI that does hours of professional work inside a company’s systems. Its evidence ranges from Eli Lilly to AIG to Jane Street.

## 2. Who Controls Anthropic: The Share Classes, Simply

**Buyers of Anthropic’s public shares will own a slice of the profits but will not control the company.** Two single shares carry the control.

| Class                 | Who holds it                                              | Economic rights | Voting power                                  |
| :-------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------: | :-------------------------------------------- |
| **Class A**           | Public investors, employees, existing holders             |       Yes       | 1 vote per share; **49.9% of votes combined** |
| **Class F** (1 share) | Founder LLC: the 7 co-founders                            |      None       | **A fixed 50.1% of shareholder votes**        |
| **Class T** (1 share) | Long-Term Benefit Trust                                   |      None       | **Elects the majority of the board**          |
| **Class E**           | Employees (excluding co-founders)                         |      None       | Votes on certain limited matters only         |
| **Class N**           | Strategic partners (Amazon, AMD), the Public Benefit Fund |       Yes       | No vote                                       |

![Diagram: the Long-Term Benefit Trust elects the board majority, the Founder LLC holds 50.1% of shareholder votes, and Class A holders hold 49.9%](/anthropic-s1/anthropic-who-controls.jpg)

In practice, at the shareholder meeting, the founders’ single Class F share outvotes every public share combined. In the boardroom, directors chosen by the trust hold the majority. The trust and the Founder LLC have agreed to vote together on board composition, and “collectively… will have the ability to elect all members” of the board.

The **Long-Term Benefit Trust** <Info label="Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT)">A Delaware “purpose trust”, not a charity, created to make sure Anthropic balances shareholder returns, its impact on others, and its mission. Its trustees may not own Anthropic stock and are paid a fixed cash fee.</Info> has five trustee seats, one vacant. It is chaired by Dr. Neil “Buddy” Shah, CEO of the Clinton Health Access Initiative. Trustees serve annual terms with no limit, choose their own successors, and may not hold Anthropic stock.

**The founder vote has an end date built in.** The Class F share disappears once fewer than three co-founders remain active for 90 consecutive days, or if a supermajority of them redeems it.

Because the trust elects the board majority, Anthropic qualifies as a **“controlled company”** under Nasdaq rules. It says it will use that exemption only for its nominating committee.

**The board today** has six directors:

- **Elected through Classes A and F:** Dario Amodei (CEO), Daniela Amodei (President and Chair), and Yasmin Razavi of Spark Capital.
- **Trust-elected (Class T):** Reed Hastings, Christopher Liddell and Vas Narasimhan.

## 4. The Total Addressable Market, According to Anthropic

**Anthropic frames its market as knowledge work itself, which it values at more than $40 trillion a year.**

| Market-sizing claim in the S-1                                  | Figure                     | Source cited       |
| :-------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------- | :----------------- |
| Value of knowledge work, globally, 2025                         | **more than $40 trillion** | Anthropic estimate |
| Cumulative economic impact of AI solutions and services by 2031 | **$22.5 trillion**         | IDC                |
| Knowledge workers worldwide by 2028                             | **more than 1 billion**    | IDC                |
| Global pharmaceutical spend, 2026                               | **more than $2 trillion**  | IQVIA              |
| IT spending, 2006 → 2025                                        | $1.2T → $4.3T (~7% a year) | cited in filing    |
| Revenue of today’s 10 largest tech companies, 2000 → 2025       | $68B → more than $2.5T     | cited in filing    |

The historical comparison carries the argument. Over the same 25 years that global GDP rose from about $34 trillion to more than $118 trillion, the ten largest technology companies grew revenue about **38 times**. The filing calls that roughly 11 times the rate of GDP growth. Anthropic’s claim is that frontier AI expands the market it enters, the way cloud computing grew from nothing to $1.9 trillion of spending.

How to read it: a $40 trillion figure is the value of the work, not money that will flow to Anthropic. The filing’s own penetration data are a better guide. **Over 50% of the Fortune 500 already pay Anthropic $100,000 or more a year, yet that spending averages less than 0.1% of those companies’ total expenses.** Its ten largest Fortune 500 customers spend under 1%, about eight times the average.

## 5. How Anthropic Sees Itself and the Frontier Industry

**The filing positions Anthropic as the company defining the frontier, and frontier AI as an industry where demand will outrun supply for years.**

How it describes the industry:

- **Scaling laws** <Info label="Scaling laws">The finding that AI models improve predictably as they get more compute, data and size. The foundational 2020 paper was co-written by several of Anthropic’s co-founders.</Info> are “the primary engine of AI progress.” Several co-founders wrote the foundational paper.
- **Demand is limited by compute.** The filing expects demand for AI to “outstrip supply for the foreseeable future, limited principally by the availability of compute,” and concentrated at the frontier.
- **The end state** is “a country of geniuses in a data center”: millions of AI agents working in parallel, available to every organization.

How it describes its own position:

- **The product benchmark:** Claude Code made autonomous coding “the reference point for measuring the progress of frontier AI,” comparable in enterprise impact to the first chat assistants. NASA engineers used it to plan the first AI-planned drive of the Mars Perseverance rover.
- **“Closing the loop”:** each Claude model accelerates the next. More than 90% of contributions to Anthropic’s codebase in September 2026 were written by Claude. Claude’s success rate on hard internal engineering problems reached about 90%, up 50 points in nine months.
- **Efficiency:** in Q2 2026 revenue grew 1,368% year on year while compute costs grew 300%. Revenue per employee rose from $1.4 million to more than $6.6 million in a year.
- **Safety as a selling point:** enterprises “prefer models that are safe and aligned.” Anthropic withheld its most capable model, **Mythos**, from general release, offering it first only to vetted organizations through **Project Glasswing**, backed by up to $100 million for cyber-defense.

On competition, it names **OpenAI and Google** as direct rivals, plus open-weight models and, above all, the “status quo”: organizations that simply do not change. It argues its edge is focus on enterprises, frontier capability, research talent and compute efficiency.

## 9. Taking a Research Lab Public as a Public Benefit Corporation

**Anthropic is building a new template: a frontier research lab that sells shares to the public while legally obliged to weigh more than profit.**

As a Delaware **public benefit corporation** <Info label="Public benefit corporation (PBC)">A for-profit company legally required to balance shareholder returns with a stated public benefit and the interests of people affected by its conduct.</Info>, its directors must balance three things: shareholders’ financial interests, those materially affected by its conduct, and its stated purpose, “the responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity.”

What makes the template new:

1. **Control is separated from economics, twice:** the purpose trust picks the board majority, and the founders hold the shareholder-vote majority. Neither share carries a dollar of economics.
2. **The trust is hard to dismantle.** Changing it takes 80% of the board and 80% of the trustees. Without trustee support, it takes founder, employee and public supermajorities, or 85% of Class A shares once the founders’ share is gone.
3. **Employees get a voice.** The new Class E shares give employees a vote on certain matters, including disputes over one trust-appointed director seat.
4. **Philanthropy is built into the capital structure:**
   - **The Public Benefit Fund:** 5% of fully diluted shares, 846,840,960 Class N shares, went to a wholly owned Anthropic Public Benefit Fund.
   - **More as the stock rises:** the board may add up to 2% more each time the stock rises 50% above the prior milestone, starting at the IPO price.
   - **Employee donations are matched in stock:** that program cost $541 million in 2025.
5. **Safety can override speed.** The filing says model releases may be delayed or restricted on safety grounds, as Mythos was, even at a cost to short-term revenue.

For investors, this is the core trade: a company whose governance is designed to keep its mission ahead of its share price. The filing says so in its risk factors, and so should anyone pricing the stock.

## 10. Depreciation and Fixed Assets

**Depreciation is still tiny, because Anthropic has mostly rented its compute. That is about to change.**

| Property and equipment ($M)                      | Dec 2024 | Dec 2025 |  Jun 2026 |
| :----------------------------------------------- | -------: | -------: | --------: |
| Servers, networking and infrastructure equipment |        — |       34 |       618 |
| Construction in progress                         |        4 |       63 |       536 |
| Gross property and equipment                     |       23 |      158 |     1,234 |
| Accumulated depreciation                         |      (4) |     (12) |      (47) |
| **Net property and equipment**                   |       19 |      146 | **1,187** |

- **Policy:** straight-line <Info label="Straight-line depreciation">Spreading an asset’s cost evenly over its useful life. A $5 million server with a five-year life is expensed at $1 million a year.</Info> depreciation over **three to five years**.
- **Depreciation expense:** $1M (2023), $3M (2024), $10M (2025), **$35M in H1 2026**. Total depreciation and amortization in H1 2026 was $97M.
- **Finance leases** began in 2026, with a weighted-average remaining term of 4.9 years at a 7.1% discount rate.

Why it matters: compute rented from cloud providers shows up as an operating expense. Leased or owned hardware shows up as an asset that is **depreciated** or amortized over time. The roughly $161 billion of Broadcom equipment leases, accounted for as finance leases <Info label="Finance lease">A lease treated as if the company bought the asset with borrowed money: the asset is amortized and interest is charged on the lease liability.</Info>, will move a large share of compute cost onto the balance sheet as it comes into service. Spread over five years, that is on the order of $30 billion a year of amortization and interest once fully in service. This is our illustration, not a company figure. It does not change the economics. It does change where the cost appears in the income statement.

## 13. Qualitative Risk Factors

**These risks are harder to measure, and the filing is unusually candid about them.**

- **Staying at the frontier.** Success depends on keeping the most capable models, against well-funded competitors including OpenAI and Google.
- **Compute supply.** The chips come from a few suppliers and a few countries, are supply-constrained, and fall under export controls.
- **Model behavior.** The filing warns models may behave in “unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways, including by taking harmful actions that no user or third party directed,” and that misuse could cause harm.
- **Litigation.** The $1.5 billion _Bartz_ authors’ settlement was approved in July 2026. Suits remain from music publishers (Concord, Universal, Sony, Warner Chappell and others), Reddit, Canadian authors and news publishers, and consumer class actions over subscription marketing.
- **Regulation.** The EU AI Act imposes systemic-risk obligations on general-purpose models. On September 24, 2026, a coalition of state attorneys general led by New York and New Jersey urged Congress to legislate on frontier AI. Anthropic supports regulation and admits it may slow its own growth.
- **Governance.** Public shareholders cannot influence a change of control, and the trust and founders may favor the mission over share price.
- **Conflicts with partners.** Amazon, Google and Microsoft are suppliers, investors, sales channels and competitors at once.
- **Key people and culture.** The company depends on its seven co-founders and research talent.

## 14. The Discount Rate and What It Means for the IPO

**Every valuation starts with the risk-free rate, and today it is high.** On October 26, 2026 the U.S. Treasury yield curve read:

| Maturity     |     Yield |
| :----------- | --------: |
| 3-month bill |     4.22% |
| 2-year       |     4.68% |
| 10-year      | **5.00%** |
| 30-year      |     5.31% |

Anthropic’s own filings give a second anchor. It discounts its leases at **7.1% to 7.5%**, the rate it uses to discount its own lease obligations. Equity investors demand more than lenders. A standard cost-of-equity <Info label="Cost of equity">The return shareholders require to own a stock, usually built as the risk-free rate plus a premium for risk. It is the discount rate applied to the company’s future cash flows.</Info> estimate (risk-free rate plus beta times an equity risk premium) gives roughly **11% to 12%**. That assumes a 5.0% risk-free rate, a beta of 1.3 to 1.6, and a 4.5% equity risk premium. Those are our illustrative assumptions; Anthropic has no trading history yet.

**Why the rate matters so much here.** Almost all of Anthropic’s value lies in profits years away, so its valuation behaves like a very long-duration bond. A rule of thumb from the perpetuity formula, value ≈ cash flow ÷ (discount rate − growth rate), makes the point. If the gap between the discount rate and long-run growth is 4 points, one extra point of discount rate cuts the value by **20%**: 1/5 vs 1/4.

**The price anchors so far:**

- **The Series H price** was $589.01 per preferred share, or **$58.90 per common share** after the 10-for-1 conversion. Across about 16.9 billion fully diluted shares, that is roughly **$1 trillion**.
- **The $2 trillion valuation reported in the press** would be about **$118 a share** before new shares are issued.
- **The earnings yield at $2 trillion:** Q2 2026 operating income of $974 million, annualized, is about $3.9 billion. That is an earnings yield of about **0.2%**, against 5.0% on a 10-year Treasury. The case for the stock is entirely about future growth.

We made the opportunity-cost argument in detail in September: [Anthropic at $2 Trillion: The IPO Has to Beat a 5.56% Treasury First](/blog/anthropic-ipo-2-trillion-valuation-opportunity-cost-treasury-yields). The filing’s numbers make growth more believable than they looked then. They do not change the arithmetic of the hurdle.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When did Anthropic file for its IPO, and what is the ticker?**
Anthropic, PBC filed a public Form S-1 with the SEC on October 26, 2026, after confidentially submitting a draft in June. It has applied to list Class A common stock on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the symbol ANT. The price range and share count are not yet set.

**How much revenue does Anthropic make?**
Revenue was $41 million in 2023, $386 million in 2024 and $4.59 billion in 2025. In the first half of 2026 it was $16.3 billion, including $11.6 billion in the second quarter alone.

**Is Anthropic profitable?**
On an operating basis, it turned profitable in Q2 2026 with operating income of $974 million, an 8% margin. It still reported a first-half 2026 net loss of $80.7 billion. Nearly all of that comes from non-cash increases in the value of convertible notes and other instruments as the company’s valuation rose.

**Who controls Anthropic after the IPO?**
Two single shares carry control. The Long-Term Benefit Trust holds one Class T share that lets it elect the majority of the board. The seven co-founders hold one Class F share, through a Founder LLC, with a fixed 50.1% of shareholder votes. Public Class A shareholders hold the remaining 49.9% of votes.

**How much is Dario Amodei paid?**
Dario Amodei’s 2025 compensation was $17.96 million: $654,167 of salary, $13.3 million of stock and option awards, and $4.0 million of other compensation, mostly personal security. His base salary rose to $1.38 million in July 2026. Daniela Amodei is paid the same salary and equity; her 2025 total was $16.41 million.

**How much cash does Anthropic have?**
As of June 30, 2026, Anthropic held $112.4 billion of cash and cash equivalents and $19.3 billion of short-term investments, a total of $131.7 billion. It also had $3.2 billion available under its credit facility, later replaced by a $15 billion facility.

**How much has Anthropic committed to spend on compute?**
Audited minimum compute commitments were $265.6 billion at June 30, 2026, up from $54.6 billion at the end of 2025. The filing describes arrangements with Broadcom, SpaceXAI, Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Nscale, Lambda and AMD, several signed after June 30. Some are cancellable or usage-based.

**What is a Virtual Collaborator?**
It is Anthropic’s term for AI agents that work alongside people on complex, multi-step professional tasks, using the access rights, system connections and context an organization grants them. Claude Code and Claude Cowork are the main examples today.

**What are the biggest risks in Anthropic’s S-1?**
The largest measurable risks:

- Compute commitments far larger than current revenue.
- Heavy reliance on cloud marketplaces (48% of revenue).
- Dilution from convertible notes and the Public Benefit Fund.
- Revenue that depends on frequent new model releases.

The qualitative risks:

- Competition from OpenAI and Google.
- Compute supply.
- Model misbehavior.
- Copyright litigation.
- Governance that limits public shareholders’ influence.

## Conclusion and Key Takeaways

1. **Growth:** about 10× a year, then 14× year on year in the first half of 2026. Q2 2026 revenue was $11.6 billion.
2. **Profitability:** the first operating profit came in Q2 2026, driven by compute cost falling from $2.51 to $0.48 per revenue dollar.
3. **Cash:** $131.7 billion on hand, and operating cash flow positive for the first time.
4. **The bet:** at least $265.6 billion of compute commitments, much of it financed by the suppliers themselves.
5. **Control:** a purpose trust and the co-founders hold control; public shareholders hold economics.
6. **The thesis:** Virtual Collaborators, with Eli Lilly, AIG, Jane Street, BMS and DoorDash as the evidence.
7. **The hurdle:** at the reported $2 trillion, today’s earnings yield is about 0.2% against a 5% Treasury. The price is a bet on the next decade.
