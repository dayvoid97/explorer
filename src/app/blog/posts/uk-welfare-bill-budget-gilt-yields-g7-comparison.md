## 1. The welfare bill, in numbers

Start with the scale. The DWP's latest forecast puts **£322.6 billion** of social security spending in Great Britain in 2025-26, including the State Pension (as reported by the TaxPayers' Alliance, citing DWP). The trajectory is what alarms the Treasury: the OBR's March 2026 forecast reportedly expects welfare spending to exceed **£400 billion a year by 2030-31** — that figure comes via the TaxPayers' Alliance's reading of the OBR, and we have not verified it directly against the OBR's own release, so treat it as reported rather than confirmed.

What is confirmed from the OBR itself: welfare spending was £245.2 billion in 2021-22 (10.5% of GDP) and was already forecast to reach £327.5 billion by 2027-28 (11.2% of GDP) in an earlier supplementary release. Six major benefit caseloads — Universal Credit, PIP, Housing Benefit, DLA, ESA and Carer's Allowance — grew **25.8% in cost between November 2023 and November 2025**, against median pay growth of 10.4% over the same period (TaxPayers' Alliance). The welfare bill is growing more than twice as fast as wages.

## 2. The £36 billion budget problem

The arithmetic facing Chancellor John Healey ahead of his first budget on **October 28, 2026** is brutal. Public sector net borrowing was **£18.3 billion in August 2026** — £3.5 billion above the OBR's forecast and £2.9 billion above August 2025. Borrowing for the financial year to date stands at **£77.3 billion, £8.1 billion above forecast** (ONS figures, via press reporting). Debt interest alone has cost roughly **£50 billion since April** — about £2 billion more than the OBR's March forecast (reported figures; single-source).

The Institute for Fiscal Studies, via Reuters on October 11, put the number on it: Healey needs **£36 billion of tax rises or welfare cuts** — equivalent to adding three percentage points to the main rate of income tax — just to offset the Iran-war inflation hit to public services and meet a defence-spending pledge. The IFS explicitly tied the two pressures together: the government is "under pressure to reduce Britain's rising welfare spending, especially after a sharp rise in government borrowing costs since the start of the conflict in the Gulf."

Fiscal headroom — the margin by which the Chancellor meets his own fiscal rules — is projected to shrink from under £24 billion to around **£13 billion** (FT reporting, via press). Public sector net debt stands at **93.8% of GDP** as of end-August 2026 (ONS, provisional). The context: CPI inflation at 3.1% in August, Q2 GDP growth of just 0.5%.

## 3. The gilt market's verdict

Bond investors have stopped giving Britain the benefit of the doubt. On October 8, the **10-year gilt yield hit 5.527%** — its highest since 2007. The **30-year gilt touched 6.047%**, its highest since January 1998, before easing to around 5.9% (LSEG via Morningstar/Dow Jones).

The Bank of England is holding the line but tilting hawkish: Bank Rate stays at **3.75%** after a 6-3 vote in October — three dissenters wanted 4% — and markets price roughly an 86% chance of a hike in November. As Dow Jones put it on October 8: "Weak economic growth and rising borrowing costs are constraining public finances, causing investors to add a **fiscal-risk premium** on government bonds."

The transmission to households is direct. The average two-year fixed mortgage rate is around **5.98%** (Moneyfacts, early October) — its highest since December 2023 — and sub-5% deals collapsed from 1,494 to just 9 in September (reported figures). Sterling tested **$1.3180** — its weakest since late June — as the gilt selloff "cut fiscal headroom nearly in half" (press reporting). The FTSE 100, at **10,552 (+6.25% YTD)**, was headed for its worst month since March as "rising bond yields dampened sentiment" (Reuters, October 5).

The mechanism is textbook: a rising welfare bill widens the deficit → more gilt issuance → higher yields → higher debt-service costs → less fiscal headroom → more pressure on the currency and on risk assets. Britain is living it in real time.

## 4. France: the welfare state that outspends everyone

Here is the twist the reader who sent us down this path suspected — and the data backs it. France spends roughly **32% of GDP on social protection** — the highest in the OECD (OECD, corroborated by Eurostat at 31.9% for 2024). Britain, at about **20.5%**, is not even close. France is the welfare state; Britain merely has a welfare bill problem.

And yet France's 10-year OAT yields **4.76-4.95%** — roughly 60-70 basis points *below* the UK's 10-year gilt. The spread of French bonds over German Bunds has blown out to **131-159 basis points**, the widest since the 2011-12 euro crisis, so markets are not relaxed about France either. But the outright yield is lower.

Why does the heavier welfare state borrow more cheaply? Three reasons:

- **Politics, not just arithmetic.** France's problem is legibility: the Bayrou government fell to a censure motion in September 2026, PM Lecornu's **€54 billion savings plan** for the 2027 budget was rejected by markets, and a presidential election looms in April 2027. France's deficit is projected at **5.4% of GDP** in 2026 — the largest of the 21 eurozone countries — with debt at a record **119.3% of GDP**. Markets price French political risk; they price British *fiscal* risk.
- **The ECB backstop.** The ECB's deposit rate is **2.50%** (hiked in September), and eurozone sovereigns trade with an implicit institutional floor Britain lacks.
- **Growth expectations.** The CAC 40 is **down 4.25% year-to-date** — the worst G7 performer — at 7,803. French banks sold off on sovereign-debt exposure fears. France's welfare burden is priced into its equities, not just its bonds.

The lesson: a big welfare state is sustainable if the market believes the politics can fund it. France's 32% is a known, decades-old fact. Britain's problem is the *trajectory* — a bill growing twice as fast as wages, colliding with a bond market that has lost patience.

Three patterns stand out:

1. **Everyone's yields are at multi-decade highs.** The US 10-year is at a 24-year high, Germany's at its highest since 2009, Japan's since 1996. This is a global bond selloff — driven by the Iran/Middle East oil shock (Brent around $100-105) and renewed central-bank tightening (the Fed hiked on October 7, the ECB hiked in September, the BoJ hiked to a 31-year high in September). Britain is not suffering alone.
2. **But Britain pays the most among major European sovereigns.** At 5.53%, the 10-year gilt yields more than France's OAT (4.8-4.9%), Italy's BTP (4.5-4.7%) and Germany's Bund (3.4-3.5%) — despite Britain having lower debt-to-GDP than France or Italy. That gap is the fiscal-risk premium, and the welfare bill is a large part of the story the market is telling.
3. **Equities are diverging from bonds.** Japan's Nikkei is up ~37% and Canada's TSX +12.5% even as their yields rise; France's CAC is down 4.25%. Britain's FTSE 100 (+6.25%) is middling — supported by energy and multinationals, dragged by domestic rate sensitivity.

## The bottom line

Britain's welfare bill — £322.6 billion this year, reportedly heading past £400 billion a year by 2030-31 — has collided with a bond market that no longer finances deficits on trust. The 10-year gilt at 5.53% is the market's invoice for that collision, and it is being paid by every British household remortgaging near 6%.

France shows the paradox clearly: the OECD's biggest welfare state (32% of GDP) borrows more cheaply than Britain, because its burden is old news and its politics — chaotic as they are — sit inside the eurozone's institutional frame. Britain's burden is *growing*, its fiscal headroom is evaporating, and its next budget is sixteen days away.

The question for the October 28 budget is not whether the welfare bill gets addressed. It is whether the address is credible enough to make 5.53% look like a peak rather than a waypoint.
