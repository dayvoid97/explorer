> **Key takeaways**
>
> **The power is real, and it is leverage power.** From January 3, Democrats unilaterally control committees, hearings, subpoenas, the floor schedule — and Senate confirmations. That is the complete list of what a majority alone can do.
>
> **Everything else needs a signature.** A law requires 60 Senate votes to break a filibuster and President Trump's signature. A veto override requires **290 House votes and 67 Senate votes**. Democrats hold at least 54 Senate seats — **13 short of 67** — and are dozens of seats short of 290 in the House (Reuters).
>
> **Where the agenda and the farmer vote align.** Restoring the expired ACA premium tax credits is the Democrats' named first item — and self-employed farmers are among the heaviest users of the ACA marketplace. Medicaid and rural hospitals, and the intent to undo the tariff regime, also map directly onto flipped-state needs.
>
> **Where the gap is.** No stated Democratic programme was found in the agenda reporting for fertilizer (**up 15.3%** in USDA's 2026 forecast), fuel and oils (**up 28.8%**), or farm credit — in a year when Chapter 12 farm bankruptcies reached **315 filings in 2025, up 46%**.
>
> **The calendar problem.** The Farm Bill expired September 30. Federal funding runs out **December 11** and farm programmes revert to 1930s–40s permanent law on **December 31** — both in the lame-duck session of the *outgoing* Republican Congress. The new Democratic majority is seated **January 3**. It inherits the outcome; it does not cast that vote.

## What the new majority says it will do

The stated Democratic agenda is on the record, from its leaders, before and after the win.

House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries: Democrats "plan to focus on driving down the high cost of living, lowering the price of groceries, gas and housing, and fighting to make healthcare affordable for every American" — "We are explicitly promising to fight for an affordable America." Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer said the party would "focus on affordability and keeping Trump and his administration in check… do everything we can to lower costs… clamp down on this horrible corruption and to end the chaos." After the win, Jeffries' refrain remained "America is too expensive," and Schumer, in his victory speech, said "Relief is on the way" (Reuters; USA Today).

The operational version, reported by AP in October, is a first-100-hours / first-100-days list:

1. **Reinstate the ACA premium tax credits** that expired in 2025, leaving millions facing higher health-insurance costs.
2. **Reverse the Medicaid cuts** in the One Big Beautiful Bill, including its new work requirements. Reuters quantifies the target as undoing **$900 billion** in Medicaid cuts; Jeffries has pledged to pass legislation reversing them.
3. **Hold votes on tariffs and the Iran war** — undoing the president's tariff regime and challenging the war's cost effects.
4. **Oversight** — investigations of the administration, including its dealings with corporate executives, using the subpoena power that comes with committee control.
5. Five **affordability working groups**: healthcare, housing, groceries and goods, gas and utilities, and caregiving.

Incoming Senate Finance Chair Ron Wyden added on November 4 that his committee would "reassert congressional authority over our country's trade policy" (Reuters).

That is the promise. The next section is the need it has to answer.

## What the voters who flipped actually need — the farmer ledger

The flipped Senate seats in this packet — Kansas, Iowa, Texas, Ohio, the Carolinas, Maine — include the states where the farm vote moved. USDA's own September 2026 forecast describes the economy those voters were voting in:

- **Net farm income: $158.4 billion in 2026**, down $4.3 billion, or 2.6%, from 2025 — and down 5.5% after inflation.
- **Total production expenses: $492.8 billion**, up $21.2 billion, or 4.5%.
- **Fertilizer, lime and soil conditioners: $39.6 billion, up 15.3%.**
- **Fuel and oils: up 28.8%** — the largest percentage cost increase in the forecast.
- Direct government payments are forecast at **$47.4 billion, up nearly 70%** — meaning the income figure above already includes a large increase in federal support.
- For balance, USDA's data also shows forecast net farm income remains **above its inflation-adjusted 2006–2025 average**. The squeeze is a margin and cost story, concentrated by commodity and region. It is not an aggregate collapse, and this article does not describe it as one.

On trade, a North Dakota State University study puts lost U.S. agricultural export sales to China at **$14.9 billion from March 2025 to February 2026** under China's retaliatory tariffs — soybeans alone accounting for $6.8 billion. China's September 2026 tariff reduction on many U.S. farm products explicitly left soybeans out.

On credit, U.S. Courts data counts **315 Chapter 12 farm bankruptcies in calendar 2025, up 46%** from 2024 — the highest since 2020. Filings through September 2026 ran about 19% above the same period of 2025. Chapter 12 also understates distress by construction: it excludes farm households whose off-farm income exceeds half their total, and operations above its debt ceiling.

On healthcare, the farm vote and the hospital map are the same map. Family farmers are very often self-employed and have had very high rates of enrolment in the ACA marketplace, in the words of Dr. Katherine Hempstead of the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation — and farmers skew older, which raises premiums further. Trade and advocacy sources put more than 40% of rural hospitals operating at a loss, with roughly 700 at risk of closing. Those rural figures come from advocacy and trade sources and are labelled as such; the enrolment point is the structural one.

## The mechanics: what "power" means here

Immense power, in a divided government, resolves into three distinct kinds — and only one of them is immense on its own.

**Needs only the majority (deliverable January 3, no president, no opposition votes):** electing the Speaker and the Majority Leader; setting both floors' schedules, including what never gets a vote; committee chairs, hearings, investigations and subpoenas; blocking legislation by not passing it; and Senate confirmations — judges, a Supreme Court vacancy if one arises, Federal Reserve governors, agency commissioners. Reuters states the courts consequence plainly: after January, judicial confirmations "will slow to a trickle," after a period in which a Republican Senate easily confirmed 56 life-tenured judges in this term.

**Needs 60 Senate votes plus a signature:** the Farm Bill, ACA restoration as a standalone bill, Medicaid reversal, a tariff-authority statute, appropriations, and the debt-limit increase. Every item a farmer would feel directly in 2027 sits in this row.

**Needs an override nobody has:** two-thirds of both chambers — **290 of 435 in the House, 67 of 100 in the Senate**. At least 54 Democratic senators is 13 short. Reuters' conclusion is one sentence: Democratic majorities "will not be large enough to override his decisions."

What that leaves is the fourth instrument, the one both parties' analysts named first: **the purse as a bargaining table.** Congress controls fiscal 2027 funding — with a December 11 deadline that falls in the lame duck — and the debt limit, whose fight begins late next year. Reuters, quoting Natixis chief U.S. economist Christopher Hodge, describes a credible shutdown or debt-ceiling threat as the lever that extracts concessions, and names restoring health-insurance tax subsidies as the example concession. That is leverage power: real, consequential, and transactional. Every win it produces arrives inside somebody else's must-pass bill, negotiated — not enacted on a party-line vote.

And the lame-duck inversion sits underneath all of it. The Farm Bill cliff (December 31), the funding cliff (December 11), and the planned confirmation push all fall **between November 9 and January 3, while Republicans still hold both gavels**. The new majority's first act on farm policy is not a vote. It is inheriting whatever the outgoing Congress did, or failed to do, in December.

## Fact box — Kentucky: the flip that was, and the one that wasn't

"Kentucky flipped" circulated widely on election night. The verified record is narrower, and more interesting.

> **Senate — did NOT flip.** Andy Barr (R) defeated Charles Booker (D) to succeed the retiring Mitch McConnell, by about **55% to 43%** on unofficial results — **784,867 votes to 612,349** on the AP table, with 99% counted. AP called the race at about 9:15 p.m. ET. Kentucky voted for Trump by about 30 points in 2024. Barr enters the Senate in the minority.
>
> **House, KY-06 — DID flip.** Zach Dembo (D), a Navy veteran and former federal prosecutor, defeated Ralph Alvarado (R) in Central Kentucky's 6th District by **52.7% to 43.3%** — **138,777 votes to 114,030** on the AP table — in a district that **voted for Trump by 15 points in 2024** and had been held by Republicans since 2012. Dembo campaigned on affordability, reversing blanket tariffs and the war in Iran as price drivers, and reversing the Medicaid and food-assistance cuts. He becomes the second Democrat in Kentucky's House delegation.
>
> **The accurate sentence is:** Kentucky flipped one central-Kentucky House seat by about 9 points in a Trump-plus-15 district, and kept its Senate seat Republican by about 12 points. Any version shorter than that is wrong in one direction or the other.

## The responsibility, stated mechanically

With both gavels, Democrats now co-own the consequences of the calendar. A lapsed programme in the new Congress, un-extended ACA credits after January 3, or a shutdown in a funding fight their own leverage provoked can no longer be recorded as one party's obstruction alone — that is a statement about shared mechanical responsibility, not a judgement on any party's platform.

The measurement the flipped-state voters supplied is not complicated, and this packet has recorded it state by state: a passed Farm Bill, a fertilizer bill, a diesel bill, a premium bill a family can pay. The new Congress's stated agenda answers the last of those directly, the trade question in intent, and the first three not yet at all. Its certain powers are to block, to investigate, and to bargain. What it bargains *for* — and whether the farm lines are in the deal when the December and debt-limit tables are set — is the record the next two years will be read against.
