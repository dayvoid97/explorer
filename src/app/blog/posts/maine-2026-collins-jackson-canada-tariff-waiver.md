## 1. The Result

Troy Jackson, a former president of the Maine Senate and a fifth-generation logger, defeated five-term Sen. Susan Collins, **53.8% to 46.1%** with about 86% of the expected vote counted (NBC). Collins conceded. Both candidates come from Aroostook County, the remote northern county best known for potatoes and timber.

Jackson and his allies spent the campaign painting Collins as beholden to the president (NPR). Prediction-market traders on Kalshi had priced Jackson at about **65%** to win before the vote.

## 2. The Tariffs and the Waiver

Maine shares a long border and a deep supply chain with Canada: fertilizer, farm machinery, packaging, lumber and road salt flow south; blueberries, potatoes, seafood and paper flow both ways.

**The tariffs.** Over the summer, the administration imposed **50% tariffs** on about **$20 billion** of Canadian goods (CNBC, BNN Bloomberg).

**The waiver.** On **October 23**, the president signed a proclamation exempting some Canadian imports. Collins announced it. It covered nine categories she tied to Maine's **blueberry, potato, apple and seaweed** producers, including:

- agricultural machinery
- plywood
- seaweed
- packaging materials
- refrigeration equipment

The White House described the move as a response to Canada's trade actions; AP's account was that it came as the president sought to help Collins in a tough re-election race (PBS/AP). The president also said other Canadian products would replace those taken off the list (CNBC).

## 3. The Argument Over Credit

The waiver became the campaign's closing argument, from both sides.

- **Collins** pointed to the exemptions as proof she could deliver for Maine, and said she had also lobbied for relief on concrete, pulp and road salt.
- **Jackson** said the credit was overstated and that Collins deserved blame for enabling the tariffs in the first place. "We can't eat road salt," he said of the list.

Bloomberg's election-day report on the race ran under the headline "Screwed either way." Relief on nine categories still left the rest of the 50% tariffs in place.

## 4. What Maine Tells Us About Tariff Politics

**A targeted exemption is a weaker message than a policy.** Voters who depend on cross-border trade heard that relief was possible, and that it depended on a single decision that could be reversed. That framing favored the challenger.

**Tariffs were a national issue too.** Most voters told CBS News exit pollsters the tariffs had hurt the economy. In Kansas, the winning Democrat ran on ending them outright (see [Did Farmers Flip?](/blog/did-farmers-flip-2026-midterms-iowa-kansas-farm-vote)).

**What comes next is in Congress's hands.** House Democrats have said they will move quickly on legislation to end the global tariffs and restore Congress's authority over trade. Without a veto-proof majority, Maine's industries should expect negotiation, not an overnight change (see [The Democratic Congress: Priorities and the Farm Gap](/blog/democratic-congress-2027-agenda-farmers-tariffs-farm-bill)).

## Conclusion and Key Takeaways

1. **The waiver was real, narrow and late**: nine categories, eleven days before the vote.
2. **The argument over credit decided its value**, and the challenger's version won.
3. **Maine is the clearest case of tariffs as a local issue**: a border economy where trade is the household budget.
4. **Relief now runs through Congress**, where Democrats hold the agenda but not a veto-proof majority.
