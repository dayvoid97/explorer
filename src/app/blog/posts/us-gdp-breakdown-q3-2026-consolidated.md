**Financial Gurkha | New York City | November 25, 2026**

The Bureau of Economic Analysis revised third-quarter US GDP growth up to 4.3 percent at an annual rate this morning, from 3.4 percent in the advance estimate — the fastest pace in three years, and nearly double the second quarter's 2.2 percent. The standard way of reporting that number splits the economy into five lines: consumer spending, investment, government, exports and imports. This breakdown consolidates it instead, because the five lines are not five separate economies. Imports are not a sector; they are the part of consumption, investment and government spending that happened to be produced abroad, subtracted at the end so the total measures production rather than spending. Exports are the mirror image — foreign buyers doing their own consuming and investing with American output. Rebuilt that way, the quarter tells a sharper story: demand aimed at the US economy grew at a 6.9 percent pace, investment supplied more than half of it, and three-fifths of the net result leaked back out through imports — most of them the hardware of the AI buildout itself.

## The Quarter in One Ledger

Start with the whole economy on one page. In the third quarter, buyers directed spending equal to 115.1 percent of GDP at the US economy: 103.7 points of it from Americans themselves — consumers, businesses and governments — and 11.4 points from foreign buyers purchasing US exports. Of that combined spending, 15.1 points bought goods and services produced abroad. What remained is GDP: $33.01 trillion at an annual rate, 100 percent US production, of which 88.6 percent was bought at home and 11.4 percent was sold overseas.

The growth arithmetic works the same way. The four spending categories together generated a demand impulse of 6.88 percentage points — 160 percent of the growth that was ultimately reported. Imports subtracted 2.60 points of it. The economy kept the rest: 4.3 percent growth, the strongest since 2023, achieved while absorbing the largest trade drag in six quarters. The cleanest single gauge of what happened underneath, real final sales to private domestic purchasers — consumer spending plus fixed investment, nothing else — rose 5.8 percent, the fastest pace since the second quarter of 2021. Real gross domestic income, the income-side cross-check on the same economy, rose 4.4 percent and confirmed the print.

## Who Spent: The Gross Demand Impulse

Of the 6.88 points of demand growth generated in the quarter, investment supplied 3.74 points, or 54.4 percent. Consumer spending supplied 2.78 points, or 40.4 percent. Government supplied 0.97 points, or 14.1 percent. Foreign buyers subtracted 0.61 points, a negative 8.9 percent share, as exports fell. Domestic purchasers on their own generated 7.49 points of demand — 174 percent of the final growth figure — before the import leak and the export downturn took their share.

Measured against the net 4.3 percent instead, the same contributions read as 87.0 percent from investment, 64.7 percent from consumers, 22.6 percent from government, negative 14.2 percent from exports and negative 60.5 percent from imports. Both presentations are in this report because they answer different questions: the first shows who created the demand; the second shows what the reported growth was made of after the leak.

## Consumer Spending: Two-Thirds of the Economy, Two-Fifths of the Push

Consumer spending is 67.6 percent of GDP — $22,312.8 billion at an annual rate, of which services account for $15,391.3 billion and goods for $6,921.5 billion. It grew 4.2 percent at an annual rate in real terms, up from 3.8 percent in the second quarter, and the second estimate revised it up from the 3.8 percent first reported. In nominal dollars, spending is up 6.1 percent from a year earlier.

### Where the consumer's growth came from

The upward revision was a goods story: pharmaceuticals, recreational goods and vehicles — a category that includes computer software and accessories — and gasoline all came in stronger than first measured, on revised Census retail data and new Energy Department figures. Services were revised the other way, led down by health care on new Census Quarterly Services Survey data. Health care remains the largest single services line in the economy at $3,858.7 billion, 11.7 percent of GDP on its own. The consumer's import content, notably, did not surge: imports of consumer goods excluding food and automobiles were $700.5 billion, down 3.8 percent from a year earlier. The American consumer spent more in the third quarter, and a slightly larger share of it stayed home than the headline trade figures suggest.

## Investment: The Boom and Its Import Shadow

Investment is 18.6 percent of GDP — $6,141.7 billion — and in the third quarter it behaved like an economy twice that size. It contributed 3.74 points of growth, more than the consumer, split between fixed investment at 2.10 points and a swing in private inventories worth 1.64 points, as stocks went from a $95.8 billion drawdown in the second quarter to a $21.9 billion build. Fixed investment grew 12.0 percent at an annual rate, its fastest since the fourth quarter of 2020. Business investment grew 16.1 percent, its fastest since the reopening quarter of 2020, with equipment up 21.8 percent, intellectual property products up 13.6 percent — software alone accounting for roughly two-thirds of that — and structures up 9.5 percent. Residential investment was the one decline, down 3.4 percent.

### The AI line item, in dollars

The buildout is now legible in the national accounts. Spending on computers and peripheral equipment ran at a $504.1 billion annual rate in the third quarter, up 70.5 percent in dollars from $295.7 billion a year earlier. Information processing equipment overall reached $933.9 billion, up 40.8 percent year over year. Investment also led the quarter's big revision: of the 0.9-point upgrade from the advance estimate, the largest share came from intellectual property — and within that, from a Bureau of Labor Statistics revision to software prices. Part of the strongest growth print in three years is a measurement correction, which is worth knowing before treating 4.3 percent as pure momentum.

### The shadow

The same boom is the import story. Capital goods imports — the servers, computers and machinery flowing into the buildout — ran at $1,782.9 billion in the third quarter, up 59.8 percent from a year earlier. Imports of computers, peripherals and parts alone were $751.3 billion, up 110.7 percent year over year, and larger than the entire domestic computer investment line they feed. In September, capital goods imports hit a record $158.0 billion in a single month, and analysts estimated computers, peripherals and semiconductors accounted for roughly 61 percent of that month's trade deficit. Consolidated, the transaction reads honestly on both sides: investment demand up 3.74 points, of which a substantial share was produced abroad and returned through the import line.

## Government: The Quiet Quarter of Support

Government consumption and investment is 17.5 percent of GDP — $5,772.6 billion — with state and local governments the larger half at $3,713.5 billion and the federal government at $2,059.1 billion, including $1,233.2 billion of defence. After contributing roughly nothing in the second quarter, government spending grew 5.7 percent at an annual rate in the third and added 0.97 points to growth — 22.6 percent of the net increase, and 14.1 percent of the gross demand impulse. The acceleration was federal: defence spending rose 11.4 percent annualised and nondefence 23.2 percent. In nominal dollars, government is up 6.8 percent from a year earlier. It was not the quarter's headline, but it was the swing factor versus the spring: remove it, and the print is closer to 3.3 percent than 4.3.

## Exports: Foreign Buyers Stepped Back

Exports are 11.4 percent of GDP — $3,752.1 billion — and they are best understood as the rest of the world's consumption and investment directed at American production. The basket mirrors a domestic economy's: capital goods of $849.7 billion, including $144.9 billion of computers and $179.6 billion of civilian aircraft; industrial supplies of $861.1 billion; services of $1,323.5 billion, the category where the US runs its surplus; consumer goods of $265.0 billion; food of $181.9 billion; and automobiles of $155.4 billion. In the third quarter that foreign bid shrank: exports fell 5.4 percent at an annual rate, more than reversing the second quarter's gain, and subtracted 0.61 points from growth. The level is still up 10.7 percent in dollars from a year earlier — this was a weak quarter for foreign demand, not a weak year.

## Imports: Not a Sector — a Leak Inside the Others

Imports totalled $4,969.2 billion, 15.1 percent of GDP, and rose 19.2 percent at an annual rate — subtracting 2.60 points from growth, the largest single drag in the report. Consolidated into the spending they belong to, the composition is the finding of the quarter. Capital goods were $1,782.9 billion of the total and consumer goods excluding food and automobiles were $700.5 billion — and those two lines moved in opposite directions, capital goods up 59.8 percent year over year while consumer goods fell 3.8 percent. Food imports were $218.5 billion, industrial supplies — intermediate inputs that feed production generally rather than any single final category — were $683.6 billion, automotive and other goods about $634.3 billion split between households and businesses, and services imports $949.3 billion, a mix of household travel and business transport and intellectual property charges. The leak, in other words, is not broad-based. It is concentrated in exactly one place: the imported hardware inside the investment boom. September's trade data, the last month of the quarter, showed imports at a record $426.6 billion with the goods deficit at $105.0 billion — and the driver named in the release was capital goods, again.

## What the Consolidated Ledger Says That the Headline Does Not

Three conclusions survive the consolidation that the standard five-line table blurs. First, this was a demand boom, not a production miracle: purchasers generated growth impulses worth 160 percent of the reported figure, and the economy's constraint was how much of that demand could be met from domestic production. Second, the boom's centre of gravity has moved from the consumer to the capital budget — an 18.6 percent slice of the economy supplied 87 percent of net growth, something that has not been true of a non-recession-recovery quarter in decades. Third, the trade deficit and the investment boom are currently the same phenomenon photographed from two sides: the fastest-growing import lines are the capital goods the boom requires, up 59.8 percent year over year against a 3.8 percent *decline* in consumer goods imports. Any reading of the quarter that treats the trade drag as foreign weakness, or the investment surge as purely domestic strength, is missing the consolidated picture. The third and final estimate of third-quarter GDP arrives on December 23.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why are imports subtracted in GDP if they are part of spending?**
Because GDP measures production, not spending. Consumption, investment and government figures count everything those buyers purchased, including goods made abroad. Imports are subtracted once, in total, to remove the foreign-produced share — leaving only what was produced inside the United States.

**What percent of Q3 2026 GDP growth came from investment?**
Investment contributed 3.74 of the 4.3 percentage points of growth — 87.0 percent of the net increase, and 54.4 percent of the 6.88-point gross demand impulse before imports were netted out.

**How much of US GDP is consumer spending?**
67.6 percent in the third quarter of 2026: $22,312.8 billion of the $33,010.0 billion total, at annual rates.

**Did imports grow because consumers bought more foreign goods?**
No. Consumer goods imports excluding food and automobiles fell 3.8 percent year over year. The import surge was capital goods, up 59.8 percent, and computers specifically, up 110.7 percent — the import content of the AI investment buildout.

**Is the 4.3 percent figure year over year?**
No. It is the change from the second quarter, expressed at an annual rate; the economy grew 1.1 percent within the quarter itself. In nominal dollars, GDP is up 5.9 percent from the third quarter of 2025.
